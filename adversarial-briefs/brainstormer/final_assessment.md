# Brainstormer Final Assessment

## Final Scorecard

**Overruling *Miller* (the QP as granted): Respondent.** The stare decisis fortress held all three rounds. Petitioner's chronicle/ledger successor rule was the best available answer to "what replaces the doctrine," but Respondent's final I.B exposed its seams fairly: nearly every consumer record is both ledger and chronicle, and a regime under which *Miller* is "overruled" while *Miller*'s outcome "could survive" reads as relitigation, not replacement. Add *Carpenter*'s express refusal to disturb *Miller* only eight years ago and the SCA's legislative reliance, and a realistic Court does not take the wrecking ball.

**Was the collection a search: Petitioner, narrowly.** The duration concession Respondent had to make ("current," no end date, fixes "for the next several days") pushes this record past *Hammond* and *Riley*, which Petitioner ultimately distinguished cleanly (hours-long fugitive locates; *Hammond*'s exigency overlay; *Riley*'s pre-*Carpenter* *Skinner* pedigree). Respondent's footnote-pointlessness argument is the best pure-reasoning passage in either brief, but it proves only that the question is open — and on an open question, an open-ended, multi-day, government-commanded stream sits closer to *Carpenter*'s "whole of physical movements" than to a *Knotts* tail. The Rule 41(b)(4)/§ 3117 structural point seals it.

**Exigency: Petitioner, decisively.** The October 8/October 10 timeline is the immovable fact of the case. Exigency is measured when the warrantless act is undertaken (*McNeely*); the only act was the October 8 fax, signed two days before any crime scene existed, by an officer frozen out of the very manhunt invoked to justify it. Respondent's *Karo* episode-by-episode answer was the best doctrinal anchor available, but *Karo* parsed what monitoring *revealed*, not which transmissions were separately *justified* — "what did the government do on October 10? Nothing" wins the colloquy.

**Remedy/suppression: Respondent, narrowly.** Petitioner's § 2708 textual answer ("nonconstitutional violations") neutralized Respondent's lead citation, and *Leon*/*Franks* forecloses good faith for a falsified certificate. But Respondent's third ground survives: the rangers found the scene independently, the trial maps rested on a distinct historical dataset, and the realistic ceiling is a fruits remand — where independent source and attenuation likely preserve most of the conviction.

**Bottom line.** A realistic Court answers the QP for the Government — *Miller* is reaffirmed, *Carpenter*-style, as a doctrine the Court trims rather than topples — while holding 6-3 or 7-2 that this open-ended real-time tracking was a search unsupported by exigency. Morgan gets a vacatur and a fruits remand; given the rangers' independent discovery and the trunk search's attenuation questions, the conviction more likely than not survives. Each side wins the question it actually built its brief around: Respondent the doctrine, Petitioner the conduct.

## How Each Brief Evolved

**Petitioner** opened with the right architecture (three nested rings) and the best narrative ("a stalker with a badge"), but Round 1 overreached on "GPS-grade" precision, left *Knotts* barely answered, and offered no successor rule. Round 2 was the transformation: the Statement's two-datasets untangling disciplined every downstream fight, the chronicle/ledger rule made Part III competitive, and the October 8 timeline converted exigency from a one-sentence flank into the brief's strongest section. Round 3 landed the remaining punches — *Hammond*/*Riley* by name, the no-fresh-acquisition severability close, the § 2708 paragraph — while subtracting overdrawn rhetoric.

Petitioner's best move was seizing exigency through chronology: "what was the emergency on October 8th?" turned Respondent's cheapest exit into Petitioner's surest ground. The weakness never cured is structural and was probably incurable: the winning ground (Parts I, II, IV) is precisely the ground on which the Court need not overrule *Miller*, and the chronicle/ledger line still wobbles under a five-records-in-a-row hypothetical. Petitioner wins the judgment and loses the QP — a tension built into the grant itself.

**Respondent** began with sound off-ramp design undermined by record-shading ("a handful of pings," "a swamp and a gas station") and a genuinely miscast *Kimble*. Round 2 was a disciplined candor pivot: duration conceded, *Kimble* fixed, *Hammond*/*Riley* added as affirmative federal authority, the *Knotts*-beeper answer containing the agency theory, and remedy promoted to a full Part — marred only by an I.B that rebutted Petitioner's superseded Round 1 brief. Round 3 fixed that misfire impressively, rewriting I.B against the actual chronicle/ledger rule and hedging § 2708 before the trap sprang.

Respondent's best move was the candor reinvestment itself, crystallized in the footnote-pointlessness argument and the "*Miller*'s outcome could survive" jujitsu — turning Petitioner's own workability concession into the doctrine-retention argument. The weakness never cured is October 10: severability conceded the October 8–9 illegality without ever securing a governmental *act* on October 10 to which exigency could attach, leaving the *Karo* answer one sharp question from collapse and forcing retreat to the deterrence fallback.

## Lessons of the Exercise

1. **The Statement of the Case is the highest-leverage section.** Both sides' largest Round 2 gains came from controlling chronology and untangling the two datasets before arguing law; whoever frames the record first disciplines every downstream dispute.
2. **Shading the record costs more than conceding it.** Every minimization ("handful of pings," "GPS-grade") returned as a credibility attack; every candid concession stopped generating return fire and bought authority at the margin. The candor arms race made both briefs stronger.
3. **A replacement rule converts an overruling argument from a complaint into a proposal** — and its seams become the new battlefield. Petitioner's Part III was not competitive until it offered the Court somewhere to land; Respondent's best counterattack was probing where that landing zone frays.
4. **Adverse authority is cheapest to answer the round it appears and ruinous to ignore.** *Hammond*/*Riley* unaddressed for one round was defensible; for two it would have been malpractice.
5. **Rebut the brief that exists, not the one you prepared for.** Respondent's stale I.B was the exchange's worst misfire despite being well-written — shadow-boxing a superseded argument loses the bench faster than a weak answer to a live one.
6. **Off-ramps beat headline wins.** Respondent needed any one of four independent exits; Petitioner had to run the table. Litigation architecture — severability, remedy ceilings, "at most a remand" — decided as much as doctrine did.
