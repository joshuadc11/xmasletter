# Petitioner's Answers

**Dexter Morgan v. United States, No. 850-418 — Q&A Phase**

*Petitioner answers each of the sixty questions propounded. Where a question calls for yes or no, the answer begins yes or no.*

---

## Part One: Answers to Respondent's Questions

### Q1. State, in one sentence usable by a magistrate, the rule that sorts a protected "chronicle" from an unprotected "ledger."

**Answer:** A third-party record is protected — and its compelled production presumptively requires a warrant — when it is in substance a chronicle of the customer's own constitutionally significant activities (his movements, his communications, his reading and searching) recorded as an incident of those activities; it is unprotected by the warrant requirement, and governed by ordinary *Oklahoma Press* reasonableness, when it documents the customer's voluntary commercial dealings with the record-keeping business itself — the business's performance of its side of the bargain. The magistrate's question is therefore what the record *memorializes*: conduct in the world that the business merely observed and logged (chronicle), or a transaction the customer affirmatively conducted *with* the business (ledger).

### Q2. Is a single cleared check a chronicle or a ledger, and does the answer change at ten checks, a monthly statement, or five years of statements?

**Answer:** A ledger — and no, the answer does not change at ten checks, a month, or five years, because the line is categorical by record type, not volumetric. A check is the customer's own commercial paper, affirmatively created and circulated, and the bank's record of it documents the bank's performance of a payment service. Quantity does not transmute the category; what quantity does implicate is *breadth*, and breadth is exactly what *Oklahoma Press* reasonableness polices — relevance, particularity, and the prohibition on fishing expeditions. The doctrinal measure for volume, in other words, already exists; it is the subpoena-reasonableness inquiry that governed compelled production for thirty years before *Miller* and will govern it after. That answer is deliberately symmetrical with our concession that *Miller*'s outcome could survive: *Miller* involved months of records, and they were ledgers then and now.

### Q3. Does a twelve-month credit-card statement fall on the chronicle side, and if so, what line must the issuing bank locate?

**Answer:** No. A twelve-month statement is a ledger at any duration, for the reason given in Q2: each entry documents a purchase the customer transacted through the issuer — the issuer's receivable, the issuer's performance. There is accordingly no constitutional line for the bank to locate before complying; it complies tomorrow as it did yesterday, and overbreadth objections travel under subpoena reasonableness. We do not deny that an aggregated year of purchases permits rich inferences about a life — so did the months of checks in *Miller* itself, which is precisely why prosecutors wanted them. The test is the record's character, not the inference's richness; a rule keyed to inferential richness would be the case-by-case sensitivity audit Respondent rightly says no custodian can administer.

### Q4. Is a pharmacy's one-year prescription purchase history a chronicle or the pharmacy's sales ledger — and is that consistent with Q2?

**Answer:** A ledger, and yes, deliberately consistent with Q2: each entry documents a discrete purchase the customer made *from the pharmacy itself* — the pharmacy's regulated dispensing, its side of the bargain. We give that answer knowing its rhetorical cost, because the alternative is a rule that sorts by sensitivity, which is no rule. Three cabins. First, "ledger" means outside the warrant requirement, not outside the law: subpoena reasonableness applies, and courts enforcing subpoenas for medical-adjacent records already weigh intrusiveness in that calculus. Second, Congress and HIPAA-type regimes remain free to layer protection above the constitutional floor — our whole point about floors is that statutes add to them. Third, the contrast with this case is instructive: Morgan transacted nothing with Verizon when his phone was pinged; the pharmacy customer handed the pharmacist a prescription. Voluntary dealing with the record-keeper is the ledger's signature, and it is present here and absent there.

### Q5. May a grand jury still subpoena a hotel guest registry, and how is "where a person slept, with whom, on what nights" not a chronicle?

**Answer:** Yes, by subpoena. The registry is the hotel's record of who contracted for which room on which night — a discrete, voluntary commercial dealing with the record-keeper, the paradigm ledger; it has been obtainable with legal process throughout American history, and nothing in our rule disturbs that (compare *City of Los Angeles v. Patel*, 576 U.S. 409 (2015), which concerned the *hotel's* own rights against warrantless inspection, not the guest's). The question's framing — "where a person slept, with whom" — describes the *inferences* the entry permits, not what the record memorializes. Every ledger permits intimate inferences; *Miller*'s checks revealed donations, debts, and associations. If inference-richness made a chronicle, nothing would remain a ledger — which is Respondent's own Q7 objection, and our answer to both is the same: the test is the record's character (a booking transaction), not the prosecutor's purpose.

### Q6. Is Ring doorbell footage stored on Amazon's servers a chronicle or a ledger, and whose conduct does it chronicle when it captures third parties?

**Answer:** Neither, because it is something more protected than either: it is the customer's own *content* — his papers and effects held in cloud bailment — which was never within *Miller*'s domain at all. Compelled production of a customer's stored content has always stood on different footing, from *Ex parte Jackson*'s sealed letters to *United States v. Warshak*, 631 F.3d 266 (6th Cir. 2010) (e-mail content requires a warrant notwithstanding third-party storage). A warrant is required, under current trajectory and under our rule alike. As to third parties captured in the frame: the process obligation runs to compelling the account holder's stored effects, and that obligation does not weaken because the effects depict others — a subpoenaed diary does not lose protection because it names the neighbors. Whether a depicted bystander has his *own* suppression claim is a separate standing question our rule does not need to answer here.

### Q7. Since the government's interest in every subpoenaed record is reconstructing someone's conduct, name one record category with prosecutorial value that stays on the ledger side.

**Answer:** Gladly, several: bank and credit records, brokerage statements, hotel registries, payroll and employment records, invoices, shipping manifests, casino transaction logs, corporate books — the entire documentary backbone of fraud, tax, and securities enforcement. The question's premise conflates the prosecutor's *motive* with the record's *character*. Our criterion is not "does the government hope to reconstruct conduct" — of course it does, always — but "what does the record memorialize." A cleared check memorializes the bank's payment; a brokerage confirmation memorializes the broker's execution; those records prove conduct only inferentially, through commercial dealings the customer chose to transact. CSLI memorializes nothing *except* the customer's whereabouts; a pen register log memorializes nothing except whom he called. When the record's entire content *is* the protected activity, it is a chronicle; when the record is a transaction from which activity may be inferred, it is a ledger.

### Q8. Reconcile "hard cases can be resolved as they arrive" with "a categorical line, not case-by-case sensitivity audits."

**Answer:** The same way every categorical rule is reconciled with its frontier. The line is categorical: enumerated chronicle categories (movements, communications, reading/searching) versus commercial-dealing records. What arrives case by case is the classification of genuinely novel *record types* at the margin — and once a category is classified, it is classified for every case thereafter, which is precisely how content/non-content under the SCA and testimonial/nontestimonial under *Crawford* operate. The vice we attribute to the post-*Carpenter* regime is different in kind: there is no rule at all, so every record category is relitigated under open-ended interest-balancing with *Miller* as the default against the citizen. Classifying new categories under a stated rule is adjudication; weighing each subpoena's sensitivity without a rule is discretion. We promise the former and condemn the latter, and there is no contradiction between them.

### Q9. Yes or no: under your successor rule, would Miller itself — same subpoena, same checks — come out the same way?

**Answer:** Yes, in result — the subpoena would be enforced, because checks and deposit slips are ledgers and the subpoena was reasonable. But not in reasoning, and not in what the depositor *is*: under *Miller* he stood wholly outside the Fourth Amendment, without any cognizable interest, entitled to nothing; under our rule he stands inside it, protected by constitutional reasonableness limits he may invoke against the overbroad or harassing demand. We made this concession in the brief on purpose and we stand on it: an honest successor rule decides cases by its criterion, not by hostility to the case it replaces.

### Q10. If Q9 is yes, what concrete holding of Miller — not its rationale — are you asking the Court to overrule?

**Answer:** This holding: that a depositor "takes the risk, in revealing his affairs to another," and therefore possesses *no* legitimate expectation of privacy — no Fourth Amendment interest at all — in any record held by a third party, 425 U.S. at 442–43. That is not dictum or mood music; it is the rule of decision in *Miller*, the holding that decided *Smith*, and the proposition the Government cites in every digital-records case to keep the citizen outside the Amendment by default. Overruling it changes the law concretely even where outcomes converge: the *Miller* depositor gains a cognizable interest and with it the ability to challenge unreasonable compelled production — something *Miller* held he could not do — and in every novel record category, the default flips from "unprotected unless this Court intervenes" to "classified under a rule." A judgment can be right and its rule of decision wrong; we ask the Court to retire the rule.

### Q11. Name one decision that "overruled" a precedent while leaving its result intact; if you cannot, why is this overruling rather than Carpenter-style trimming?

**Answer:** We will not pretend to a citation we do not have: we cannot name a case in which this Court used the word "overruled" while formally reaffirming the judgment as good law, and we do not need one. The closest kin is *Katz* itself, which declared *Olmstead*'s trespass rationale "can no longer be regarded as controlling," 389 U.S. at 353 — discarding the governing rule while leaving the old judgments undisturbed in their graves. If the Court prefers to style our request "holding that the third-party doctrine no longer states the governing rule," we accept the label without complaint, because the question presented asks whether to "invalidate the third-party doctrine," and the doctrine is the rationale — the talisman, not the result. What distinguishes this from *Carpenter*'s trimming is the default: *Carpenter* left the doctrine standing as the rule, with one exception carved from it, so the citizen loses every novel case until this Court grants certiorari again. We ask that the doctrine cease to be the default. That is a real difference, whatever the disposition is called.

### Q12. Yes or no: is Smith v. Maryland overruled outright under your rule, and why does your brief never say so?

**Answer:** Yes. A pen-register log is a chronicle of the customer's communications — its entire content is whom he called and when — and it falls on the protected side; prospectively installed, it is also surveillance under our Part I logic regardless of the third-party doctrine. And we reject the premise that the brief conceals this: the brief asks the Court to invalidate "the doctrine," identifies *Smith*'s assumption-of-risk reasoning as "counterfeit" through Justice Marshall's *Smith* dissent, 442 U.S. at 749–50, and expressly lists "communications" among the protected chronicles. *Smith* is *Miller*'s application; it stands or falls with the doctrine, and it falls. We say so here without hedging because the alternative — a rule that protects browsing histories but not calling histories — would be indefensible, and Respondent would be the first to say so.

### Q13. Identify one concrete case that comes out differently under your rule than under Carpenter as it stands; otherwise, what special justification remains?

**Answer:** Three. *First*, *Smith* itself and every case in its line: today, a comprehensive log of whom a citizen calls or e-mails is categorically unprotected metadata; under our rule it is a protected chronicle. *Second*, compelled production of search and browsing histories: lower courts today reason from *Miller*'s default and routinely deny protection; under our rule those records — chronicles of reading and inquiry — require a warrant. *Third*, the *Miller* depositor's own position: today he cannot challenge even a nakedly overbroad dragnet subpoena for his financial life, because he has no interest at all; under our rule he can, under reasonableness. The special justification is the flip of the default: *Carpenter* "as it stands" delivers protection one record category per decade, after years of fractured circuit litigation, with the citizen losing in the interim. A doctrine whose errors can be corrected only by serial grants of certiorari is the definition of unworkable.

### Q14. Yes or no: if the Court holds under Part I that the tracking was a search, Petitioner obtains complete relief without deciding Miller's fate?

**Answer:** Yes. We have never said otherwise; the brief's architecture is three independent grounds precisely so that each can stand alone. A Part I holding — search, no exigency, suppression, remand — gives Morgan everything a judgment can give him.

### Q15. Then what principle permits or requires the Court to overrule a fifty-year precedent unnecessary to the judgment?

**Answer:** Permits, not requires — and the permission comes from the Court itself. This Court granted certiorari on, and directed the parties to brief, the question "[w]hether this Court should overturn *Miller* and invalidate the third-party doctrine." Party presentation, *United States v. Sineneng-Smith*, 590 U.S. 371 (2020), guards against courts injecting issues the parties never raised; it has nothing to say against a court answering the question it ordered briefed — the Court has done exactly this when it wished to resolve a doctrine's fate squarely rather than by attrition, see *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010) (restoring the case for reargument on whether to overrule *Austin*). Constitutional avoidance is a prudential preference, and we respect it: if the Court resolves the case on Part I or Part II alone, Morgan prevails and we will not be heard to complain. But avoidance is a reason the Court *may* stop short, not a reason it *must* — and the cost of stopping short is that every novel record category continues to be decided, by default, against the citizen, under a rationale eight Justices have at one time or another questioned.

### Q16. If the doctrine "does no work" in your own case, what live controversy supplies an Article III vehicle for invalidating it?

**Answer:** The suppression dispute itself — because "the doctrine does no work here" is *our contested characterization*, not a settled premise. The Government argues the opposite at every step: that Morgan voluntarily conveyed his location by carrying a powered phone, that the fixes are carrier business records, that *Miller* and *Smith* supply the framework. If the Court rejects our ring-two characterization — if it holds these fixes *were* conveyed business records — then the doctrine's validity becomes squarely dispositive of whether a search occurred. A defense pressed by the United States as a shield against suppression is a live controversy; a court that must choose among dispositive rationales urged by the parties is not issuing an advisory opinion. Article III demands a concrete case, which a capital judgment supplies in abundance; it does not demand that every rationale be the only possible one.

### Q17. Carpenter declined to disturb Miller eight years ago after considering the same critiques; what has changed since 2018?

**Answer:** Three things, of the kind this Court's stare decisis cases count. *First*, doctrinal: *Carpenter* itself is the change. It held the doctrine's twin rationales — voluntary conveyance and assumption of risk — fail in the digital context, 585 U.S. at 314–15. A precedent whose reasoning the Court has already declined to apply to the defining records of the era is an eroded precedent, and erosion is a classic special justification (*Janus*'s treatment of *Abood* after *Harris* and *Knox* is the template). *Second*, experiential: eight years of lower-court application have demonstrated that exception-without-rule cannot be administered — the geofence, pole-camera, and reverse-keyword litigation has fractured the circuits, each court guessing whether *Carpenter* or *Miller* supplies the default. Unworkability proven by experience is exactly what distinguishes a special justification from mere disagreement. *Third*, factual: the universalization of third-party custody — connected cars, wearables, smart homes, voice assistants — has made *Miller*'s premise (third-party records as a discrete category of commercial paper) describe nothing about how Americans now live. *Carpenter* could responsibly defer in 2018; the deferral's costs are now data, not prediction.

### Q18. The day after Miller falls, what process gets a grand jury a securities-fraud target's brokerage statements, and which side of your line do they fall on?

**Answer:** An *Oklahoma Press* subpoena — the same instrument, the same showing, the same morning routine as the day before *Miller* was decided. Brokerage statements fall on the ledger side: each entry documents a trade the customer directed and the broker executed — the broker's performance of its side of the bargain, the customer's voluntary commercial dealing with the record-keeper itself. The question's parenthetical — "a chronicle of the customer's trades" — borrows our word but not our criterion: the trades *are* transactions with the business; that is what makes the record a ledger rather than the broker's incidental observation of the customer's life. This answer is the one our brief promised, and we give it without qualification because the grand jury's documentary toolkit is exactly what our rule is built to leave intact.

### Q19. Yes or no as to each: in Knotts, (a) the beeper was government-installed, (b) nothing was voluntarily conveyed, (c) public-road monitoring was nonetheless no search?

**Answer:** Yes, yes, and yes. All three propositions are accurate statements of *Knotts*, and we accept them.

### Q20. Then state the rule under which Verizon's "agency" converts this acquisition into a search when the government's own beeper effected none.

**Answer:** There is no such rule, because agency never "converts" anything — and the question helpfully exposes why the Government's *Knotts* counter defends the wrong doctrine. Two distinct questions govern every surveillance case. *Question one* (Katz/Carpenter): did the monitoring invade a reasonable expectation of privacy — what did it reveal? *Knotts* answered that question for a one-trip beeper tail in 1983; *Carpenter* answers it for cell-phone location monitoring now, holding the expectation extends to the whole of one's physical movements. That is our Part I, and it does not depend on Verizon's identity at all. *Question two* (Miller/Smith): even if question one favors the citizen, does the third-party doctrine independently defeat the expectation because he volunteered the information? Agency answers *that* question only: nothing was volunteered, so the doctrine supplies no independent shield. So the rule is: agency removes the Government's affirmative defense; it was never offered to create the search. If the Government now says the carrier's identity is beside the point, we agree — and then the case rises or falls on question one, where *Carpenter*, not *Knotts*, supplies the rule for phones.

### Q21. Quote the language in Knotts confining its "dragnet" reservation to open-ended monitoring of a single suspect rather than mass surveillance.

**Answer:** We cannot, because no language in *Knotts* confines the reservation in either direction, and we will not manufacture a quotation. What the opinion says is that if "dragnet-type law enforcement practices" of the sort the respondent there envisioned — he had argued the decision would permit "twenty-four hour surveillance of any citizen of this country . . . without judicial knowledge or supervision" — "should eventually occur, there will be time enough then to determine whether different constitutional principles may be applicable." 460 U.S. at 283–84. The reservation is open-textured; the Government's reading (mass surveillance only) and ours (open-ended monitoring, even of one) are both consistent with its words. What resolves the ambiguity is not *Knotts* but *Carpenter*: Timothy Carpenter was a single, targeted, identified robbery suspect, and the Court nonetheless held that "different constitutional principles" — a reasonable expectation in the whole of one's movements — applied to him. 585 U.S. at 310–13. The reservation's trigger is the character of the surveillance power, not a head-count of its targets; *Carpenter* proves it, because *Carpenter* was a one-target case.

### Q22. Identify the record evidence — page and line — that Verizon "activated" GPS or generated data it would not otherwise possess.

**Answer:** We will be precise about what the record says and does not say. It does not say "Verizon activated the phone's GPS," and we do not rest on any such finding. What it says is: Doakes faxed a demand for "Location Information," time frame "current"; that "[f]or the next several days, Doakes received location data from Morgan's phone"; that the phone was "'pinged'" — the record's own word, denoting interrogation of the device on request — with a thirty-minute delivery delay; and that the suppression motion concerned "the GPS data provided by Verizon." The quarter-mile figure describes Scuteri's *trial maps*, built from "cell-site data," a different dataset — the conflation is Respondent's, not ours. And our Part II never depended on GPS-grade precision. Commanded generation is established by the structure of the transaction: a prospective reporting stream that began upon, and only because of, a police demand — fixes produced *for the government*, on a schedule the government's request set, that Verizon "would not otherwise" have compiled and delivered, *Almonor*, 120 N.E.3d 1183. Precision goes to the intrusion's depth (Part I); the on-demand, prospective character goes to conveyance (Part II). Both are in the record.

### Q23. Yes or no: if the fixes were ordinary tower registrations the network already generates, does your no-conveyance theory still apply — and what distinguishes "reporting an operational fact on request" from "generating data on command"?

**Answer:** Yes, it still applies — because what was commanded into existence was not the tower datum but the *reporting stream*. The distinction the question asks for is retrospective versus prospective. When the government asks a carrier what its records show about yesterday — which tower carried a call — it seeks an existing business record, compiled for the carrier's purposes; that is *Carpenter*'s territory, and the third-party doctrine at least speaks its language. When the government directs a carrier to interrogate a handset and report its location at intervals *going forward*, it commissions surveillance: the deliverable — a continuing series of fixes located, compiled, and transmitted for police use — is government-commissioned work product that had no existence and no business purpose before the fax arrived. The network's latent knowledge of which tower serves a handset no more makes the stream a "business record" than a hotel clerk's latent ability to watch the lobby makes his police-requested hourly reports on a guest the hotel's own ledger. The carrier in that posture is the government's instrument, whatever the granularity of what it reports.

### Q24. Yes or no: if on October 10 an officer aware of the rangers' discovery had faxed a truthful emergency certification, would the resulting fixes have been lawful?

**Answer:** Yes — and we made that concession in our brief before Respondent asked for it, because it is what our rule entails. An officer who, confronting an interrupted homicide by a serial killer fled and unlocated, truthfully certifies a danger of death and requests current location information acts within the genuine-exigency exception, which our position leaves entirely untouched. That is where the Fourth Amendment houses its flexibility — in the exigency doctrine, not in a diluted definition of "search." The concession costs us nothing, because nothing of the sort happened: no officer made any truthful certification, or any certification, or any request at all, on October 10.

### Q25. Then your objection reduces to October 8 paperwork; why suppress identical October 10 data the government could have obtained lawfully, rather than remand for independent-source and inevitable-discovery findings — and will you oppose such findings?

**Answer:** Because "could have obtained lawfully" has never been a defense to what the government *actually did* — it is the vocabulary of inevitable discovery, a remedial doctrine with a burden attached. *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431, 444 (1984), requires the government to prove by a preponderance that the evidence *would* have been obtained through lawful means actually in motion, not that a lawful path was theoretically available; and *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988), denies "independent source" status where the decision to acquire was prompted by the unlawful acquisition. A hypothetical truthful certification no officer wrote, founded on knowledge no officer in the chain is shown to have possessed when the fixes arrived, satisfies neither standard on this record — and a rule excusing violations whenever an honest officer *could* have acted would repeal the warrant requirement for every case with probable cause. So: the October 10 fixes are suppressed as the product of the only authorization that existed, the false one of October 8. On remand, we will not stipulate to the Government's counterfactuals; we will hold it to its *Nix* and *Murray* burdens and contest the predicates the record does not support. That is not obstruction; it is the allocation of proof this Court's cases command. And to be clear about the premise: the objection is not "paperwork." The October 8 fax was the search's inception — the entire constitutional event — undertaken when no emergency existed anywhere on earth.

### Q26. Yes or no: under your no-retroactive-cure rule, could no supervening event — an abducted child, a bomb plot — ever justify continued receipt from an unlawfully opened stream, even one minute longer?

**Answer:** No, that is not our position — and the abducted child is safe under our rule. A genuine supervening emergency justifies a *fresh acquisition*, instantly: the officer who learns of the abduction may act on it that minute — a truthful certification, a telephonic request, even an oral direction to the carrier — and the fixes obtained in response to that act are lawful, because exigency excuses a warrantless governmental *act* undertaken in response to circumstances known to the officer. *Brigham City*, 547 U.S. at 404. What exigency cannot do is attach itself, by hindsight, to a stream that continues flowing solely under a false authorization while no officer takes — or, on this record, even *could* take — any responsive act, because no officer is shown to have connected the rangers' discovery to the stream before the fixes arrived. The Constitution asks one minute of one honest officer's attention; here it got neither the minute, nor the honesty, nor the officer.

### Q27. McNeely measured a single completed act; cite the case holding a continuing transmission is one indivisible undertaking, given that Karo assessed monitoring episode by episode.

**Answer:** Candidly, no case of this Court squarely holds either way for this configuration, and neither side should pretend otherwise — *Karo* included. But the materials run our way three deep. *First*, *Karo* does not establish episode-by-episode reasonableness review; it segmented by *place revealed* — in-home monitoring was a search, public monitoring was not — under the pre-*Carpenter* premise that public movements were wholly unprotected. *Carpenter* removed that premise for phone tracking: once the protected interest is "the whole of [one's] physical movements," there is no unprotected residue left to segment, and the unit of analysis is the monitoring the government set in motion. *Second*, Congress's closest analogue treats continuous electronic surveillance as a single authorized undertaking: a Title III intercept is authorized as a bounded operation, must terminate upon attainment of its objective, 18 U.S.C. § 2518(5), and unlawful interception taints its yield — no one argues each captured syllable is a separate Fourth Amendment event to be salvaged one by one. *Third*, even on the Government's episode-by-episode account, it loses: each October 10 "episode" occurred under no lawful authority and in response to no governmental act, so segmentation rescues nothing unless the exigency theory independently succeeds — and Q24–Q26 explain why it does not. Segmentation is a vehicle in search of a passenger.

### Q28. List precisely the items Petitioner contends must be suppressed, with the causal chain from the October 8 fax for each.

**Answer:** With precision, item by item:
1. **October 8–9 fixes** — direct product of the false certification; suppressed. (They were evidentially immaterial at trial, but they are the search.)
2. **October 10 fixes** (Everglades ping; service-station ping) — products of the same and only authorization; suppressed. Chain: fax → stream → fixes.
3. **The arrest and its evidentiary fruits** — the agents found Morgan at the drive-thru solely because Doakes relayed the fixes and the inferred route; chain: fax → fixes → Doakes's call to Lundy → interception. The arrest itself is an event, not an exhibit; what is suppressed is its evidentiary yield.
4. **The trunk inventory** (knives, plastic wrap, luminol) — discovered in a search incident to that arrest; fruit of item 3. The Government may argue attenuation on remand; on this record the chain is short, direct, and unbroken.
5. **The Everglades crime scene and Estrada's body** — *not* contended to be suppressible. The rangers discovered the scene before and independent of any fix; that is a genuine independent source, and we concede it (see Q30).
6. **Scuteri's trial maps** — presumptively fruit: the carrier's historical cell-site records were queried for *Morgan's* number only because the tracking located, stopped, and charged him; chain: fax → fixes → arrest → Morgan's identification as defendant → records request. The Government may attempt a *Murray*/*Nix* showing of an untainted path on remand; that is its burden, not our concession (see Q29).

### Q29. Yes or no: do you concede Scuteri's maps survive independent-source analysis, given they were built from historical records the government could have compelled on probable cause it possessed well before trial?

**Answer:** No. The question's engine — "probable cause it possessed well before trial" — is the fruit doing the laundering: the probable cause against Morgan was itself built from the tainted arrest and the trunk inventory. Before the tracking, the record shows suspicion of a Miami Metro insider generally and one stalker's fixation specifically — no probable cause as to Morgan. *Murray* requires that the *decision* to seek the evidence not be prompted by the unlawful acquisition, 487 U.S. at 542; here, no one would have pulled Morgan's carrier records had the stream not identified, located, and charged him. The taint that connects the maps to the stream is therefore investigative, not technical: the records are historical, but the *request* for them is a child of the tracking. If the Government believes an untainted investigative path to Morgan's number existed — Doakes's pre-existing suspicions ripening lawfully, the FBI's insider profile narrowing to him independently — it may try to prove that on remand, by a preponderance, as *Nix* requires. We concede the forum, not the finding.

### Q30. Yes or no: do you concede the crime scene and Estrada's body are untainted, given the rangers discovered the scene first and independently?

**Answer:** Yes. The rangers stumbled on the scene on October 10 before any fix figured in its discovery, and there is no but-for chain from Doakes's fax to their patrol. We concede the crime scene and the body are untainted, and we make the concession without reluctance, for two reasons. First, candor: a but-for chain we cannot draw is one we will not assert. Second, the concession clarifies what this case is actually about: the scene and the body prove that *someone* murdered Hector Estrada; the tainted evidence — the fixes, the arrest, the trunk, the maps the Government itself says "cinched the case" by putting Morgan "right where the murder was at the exact time of the murder" — is what made that someone *Morgan*. Suppression of the linkage evidence is not an empty remedy merely because the corpse remains.

## Part Two: Answers to the Brainstormer's Questions

### Q1. If bank records become the customer's "papers," does every third-party subpoena now require customer notice and a motion to quash — and what happens to grand-jury secrecy?

**Answer:** No notice-and-quash regime follows, on either side of our line. For ledger records — the overwhelming bulk of subpoena practice — nothing changes: no customer notice, no customer standing beyond the reasonableness interest described in Part One Q10, grand-jury secrecy intact. For chronicle records, the mechanism is the warrant, and the warrant is the *answer* to the secrecy concern, not a threat to it: the government proceeds ex parte before a magistrate, exactly as it does for every search warrant and exactly as it has done for historical CSLI since *Carpenter* — which created precisely this regime eight years ago without generating any customer-notice machinery or breaching any grand jury's secrecy. The adversarial quash fight the question fears is what our rule *avoids*: ex parte judicial authorization is more protective of investigative secrecy than litigation with the target could ever be.

### Q2. What must the custodian do with a mixed-records subpoena — classify at its own risk? What does the compliance department do the morning after?

**Answer:** The classification burden sits on the government ex ante — it must select the process the category requires — not on the custodian, who complies with facially valid process and keeps the statutory good-faith protection it already enjoys, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2703(e), 2707(e). And the morning after looks like the morning before, because carrier compliance departments *already administer exactly this kind of category line every day*: the SCA has required them for forty years to distinguish basic subscriber information from transactional records from content, each demanding different process, and *Warshak* and *Carpenter* each added a warrant category without a compliance apocalypse. The morning after our rule, the carrier's flowchart gains one branch: location and communications chronicles, warrant required — a branch most national carriers' law-enforcement manuals, post-*Carpenter*, substantially contain already. Where a custodian genuinely cannot classify a novel demand, it does what custodians do now: it asks the issuing court. That is not a constitutional crisis; it is Tuesday.

### Q3. Is the chronicle/ledger determination ex ante or ex post — and if ex ante, who adjudicates before anyone has seen the records?

**Answer:** Ex ante in operation, like every warrant requirement — and the question's premise, that someone must "see the records" to classify them, is exactly what our categorical line eliminates. Classification turns on the record *category* requested — "cell-site location records for this number" — not on the records' contents; the government knows what it is asking for before it asks. So the sequence is the familiar one: the government classifies at its peril when choosing its instrument; the magistrate rules when a warrant is sought; the issuing or supervising court rules if a custodian or recipient seeks clarification; and suppression and damages police misclassification ex post, as they police every other process error. This is the same architecture that has administered the content/non-content line for decades. Nobody adjudicates blind, because nobody needs to read a record to know it is a location log.

### Q4. Does your rule reach civil investigative demands, congressional subpoenas, IRS summonses, state administrative subpoenas — and what do the first five years look like?

**Answer:** The Fourth Amendment applies to all governmental compulsion, so yes, the rule's categories travel — but its practical reach is narrow, and we will be candid about the one real consequence. For ledgers, which are what civil agencies, the IRS, and legislatures overwhelmingly seek — financial, transactional, corporate records — nothing changes: *Oklahoma Press* and *United States v. Powell* govern as they have for eighty years. For chronicles, the consequence is real: a purely civil process cannot ordinarily support a probable-cause warrant, so protected chronicles become effectively unavailable to civil compulsion absent consent, referral to criminal process, or legislation. We do not flinch from that: it is already the law for the most intimate acquisitions — the SEC cannot wiretap, and no one thinks that an anomaly — and it has been the law for historical CSLI since *Carpenter* without administrative collapse. The first five years look like the eight years since *Carpenter*: compliance manuals updated, a handful of boundary cases litigated, and the republic's agencies still standing.

### Q5. You call Carpenter's case-by-case trimming a "slow-motion overruling," yet your hard cases arrive "as they arrive." Why is incrementalism a vice in our hands and a virtue in yours?

**Answer:** Because the increments are doing different work. The post-*Carpenter* regime is incrementalism *in lieu of* a rule: *Miller* remains the default, so every new record category is presumptively unprotected until this Court personally intervenes — and the Court must intervene category by category, forever, because the doctrine generating the wrong answers is still on the books. That is a slow-motion overruling in the precise sense that each increment exists only to countermand the rule the Court declines to retire. Our incrementalism is the ordinary application *of* a rule: the default is stated, the categories are enumerated, lower courts can decide the margins themselves, and this Court need return only when circuits genuinely split. Incrementalism that distributes decision is law working; incrementalism that requires this Court's hand on every case is the symptom of a rule that has failed. We do not begrudge the Court caution; we object to caution institutionalized as the doctrine itself.

### Q6. If a court of appeals misclassifies a category under your constitutional rule, only we can fix it; Congress can fix a statute in a session. Why prefer the less correctable allocation of error?

**Answer:** Two answers, one structural and one empirical. Structurally, the correctability argument proves far too much: it is an argument against *Katz*, against *Miranda*, against *Carpenter* itself — against the existence of constitutional criminal procedure — because every constitutional holding is "less correctable" than a statute. The Fourth Amendment's entire office is to place a floor beyond majoritarian revision; describing that feature as a defect mistakes the Amendment for a regulatory program. And Congress retains every power that matters: it can legislate above the floor, calibrate everything on the ledger side, and create the emergency pathways, immunities, and notice rules it likes. Empirically, weigh the error costs. An over-protective misclassification costs the government a warrant application — an hour, on this Court's own telling, *McNeely*, 569 U.S. at 154–55. An under-protective one leaves citizens surveilled with no recourse while the political process does what it has done for fifty years: this record *is* Congress's correction mechanism at work — a fax form, a checked box, and a stipulated-false emergency. The session in which Congress would fix that has not yet convened.

### Q7. Does the new rule apply on collateral review under Teague, and does Davis good faith insulate every conviction built on pre-decision subpoenas?

**Answer:** Answering carefully, as instructed: the rule would be a new rule of criminal procedure — not substantive, and not "watershed," a category *Edwards v. Vannoy*, 593 U.S. 255 (2021), has closed — so no, it does not apply on collateral review under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). And yes, *Davis*, 564 U.S. 229, insulates searches conducted in objectively reasonable reliance on then-binding appellate precedent, so convictions built on pre-decision subpoenas that complied with the law as it stood are secure even on direct review. We concede both points without struggle because they are load-bearing for us: together they reduce the "tens of thousands of closed cases" to zero and dissolve the retroactivity wing of the reliance objection. One caveat, so the record is clean: that insulation does nothing for *this* Government in *this* case, because Doakes relied on no precedent and no process — he falsified the predicate for the only process he invoked, and *Leon*, 468 U.S. at 923, withholds good faith from exactly that officer.

### Q8. What weight do we give the reliance of fifty states whose investigative codes assume Miller, none of them parties here?

**Answer:** Real weight, honestly acknowledged — and then three discounts the reliance cannot survive. First, *Teague* and *Davis* (Q7) wall off the entire retrospective interest: no closed state case reopens. Second, the prospective interest is smaller than it appears, because the states have been living above *Miller* for years: state high courts were the *first movers* against this doctrine — *Tracey* in Florida, *Pacheco* in Pennsylvania, *Almonor* in Massachusetts, *Weaver* in New York — and every state already administers *Carpenter*'s warrant requirement for historical CSLI. The federal floor would rise toward where much state practice already stands. Third, the bulk of state subpoena practice is ledger-side — financial, commercial, administrative records — and is untouched. As for the states' absence from the caption: that is true of every constitutional criminal procedure decision this Court has ever issued, which is why the amicus bar exists; non-party status is an argument for careful writing, not for a different Constitution.

### Q9. Carpenter promised narrowness and you ask us to overrule Miller eight years later; what is our narrowness assurance worth, and why believe your limiting language?

**Answer:** *Carpenter*'s narrowness was real and remains real — as to its *holding*. The Court promised not to decide questions not presented, and it kept that promise; it never promised that *Miller* would survive a case in which *Miller*'s fate was the question granted, and no litigant could reasonably have read it so. Assurances of narrowness bind the scope of judgments; they are not perpetual easements over future dockets. As for why our limiting language deserves belief: because it is structural, not adjectival. *Carpenter*-style narrowness is enforced by the word "narrow" — and the question shows what such words are worth. Our limit is enforced by a competing body of law that exists independently of this case: the ledger side is governed by *Oklahoma Press*, a framework older than *Miller*, administered daily, with its own case law and its own constituency. A boundary patrolled by a rival doctrine holds; a boundary patrolled by an adverb does not. That is the difference between a limiting principle and a limiting promise.

### Q10. Choose: a Katz opinion overruling Miller's expectation analysis, or a positive-law opinion grounding protection in § 2702's nondisclosure command.

**Answer:** The *Katz* opinion. Chosen without hedging: the holding we seek is that *Miller*'s secrecy-equals-privacy equation and counterfeit assumption of risk no longer state the governing rule of the reasonable-expectation inquiry. The § 2702 point keeps the subordinate role our brief gave it — one paragraph, evidentiary, corroborative: Congress's affirmative prohibition on carrier disclosure is *evidence* that the expectation society recognizes as reasonable includes nondisclosure of these records. It is a unit of proof inside the *Katz* analysis (and, candidly, an open door for any Justice who prefers the positive-law route to the same destination), not the foundation. We brief both because advocates build coalitions; we choose one because the Court asked. *Katz*.

### Q11. If Congress repealed § 2702 tomorrow, would your client's expectation survive? If not, haven't you tied the Fourth Amendment to the grace of the legislature you distrust?

**Answer:** Yes, it survives — which is the cash value of the choice just made in Q10. The expectation's source is the *Katz* inquiry as *Carpenter* applied it: the nature of cell-phone location data, its involuntariness, its comprehensiveness, the impossibility of opting out of modern life. *Carpenter* itself found the expectation reasonable without any statutory nondisclosure duty doing the work. Section 2702 is a barometer, not the weather: its existence corroborates the expectation; its repeal would be one fact among many, and a repeal *designed* to strip citizens of constitutional protection would deserve exactly the weight courts give legislative attempts to define constitutional rights down — none, as our brief says: statutes may build above the floor; they cannot lower it. So no, nothing is tied to legislative grace. The question identifies a genuine trap, and Q10's answer is why we never stepped in it.

### Q12. Was Miller wrong the day it was decided, or wrong only once technology changed? If the latter, who keeps the expiration clock?

**Answer:** Wrong the day it was decided. Its central equation — disclosure to anyone, for any purpose, forfeits protection against everyone — contradicted *Katz* from the moment of utterance: *Katz* protected words spoken into a phone line necessarily entrusted to a third-party carrier, so the proposition that entrustment forfeits protection was already falsified by the very case *Miller* claimed to apply. Justices Brennan and Marshall said so in 1976 and 1979 respectively, in real time. So there is no expiration clock and no holding that "expired on technological notice." Technology's role is the one stare decisis doctrine has always assigned changed facts: it did not make *Miller* wrong; it made *Miller*'s wrongness *intolerable* — converting an error that once misclassified a banker's box of checks into an error that misclassifies the recorded whole of American life. The error's existence is fixed at 1976; its cost is what changed. This Court has said exactly this before: "*Bowers* was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today." *Lawrence v. Texas*, 539 U.S. 558, 578 (2003).

### Q13. Is Google the government's instrument when it runs a geofence query at warrant command — is every business executing legal process "generating records on government command"?

**Answer:** In a sense, yes — and the sense is benign, which dissolves the question's worry. When a business searches its systems in answer to *a warrant*, the acquisition is attributed to the government, and the Fourth Amendment's question is already answered, because the warrant *is* the constitutional process; attribution and authorization arrive together. Our agency theory was never offered to manufacture a second search atop authorized process. Its work is done in exactly one posture: when the government procures generation or disclosure *without* judicial process and then disclaims the acquisition as a "third party's business records." Agency blocks that disclaimer; it does not haunt the compliant execution of warrants. So: Google running a geofence query under warrant is the government's instrument executing the government's authorized search — which is precisely what compelled process has always meant, and precisely why the warrant was required.

### Q14. Has CALEA conscripted every carrier as a standing government agent, and does a Title III intercept now involve two searches or one?

**Answer:** One search — the interception — exactly as before, and no standing conscription. Agency, properly understood, attaches to the execution of a *particular acquisition*, not to the maintenance of a capability: CALEA's requirement that carriers keep intercept-capable equipment makes them no more standing agents than a locksmith's duty to keep tools makes him a standing burglar. When a particular Title III intercept runs, the carrier's assistance is attributed to the government — which is not a novelty our theory introduces but the premise Title III has always embodied: that is *why* a super-warrant order is required before the carrier may lift a finger. Our theory changes nothing in that world, because that world already routes attribution through authorization. What our theory forbids is the one move Title III never permitted: the government using the carrier's hands precisely in order to claim its own were clean.

### Q15. If Verizon was the government's instrument, is it a state actor liable under § 1983 or Bivens — for every carrier complying with legal process?

**Answer:** No, and the question helpfully lets us separate two inquiries the labels blur. Fourth Amendment *attribution* asks whose search this was, so the Constitution's limits cannot be evaded by private hands; it is satisfied by government instigation and the private party's performance of the government's task — *Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Ass'n*, 489 U.S. 602, 614–16 (1989), held private railroads' drug tests were government searches on exactly that logic, without converting the railroads into damages defendants for all purposes. Damages *liability* under § 1983 or *Bivens* asks a different question — willful joint participation in unconstitutional conduct, *Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.*, 457 U.S. 922 (1982) — and carriers responding to facially valid process additionally hold statutory good-faith defenses, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2703(e), 2707(e). So: Verizon's conduct is attributed to the government for search purposes; whether Verizon could be *sued* would turn on what it knew of the falsity, and a carrier honoring a facially complete form in good faith would not be. Attribution answers "whose search"; liability answers "who pays." Different tests, different purposes, no contagion.

### Q16. If Verizon kept pinging on its own initiative after Doakes's request and volunteered the results — private search, or fruit of the original agency?

**Answer:** It depends on exactly what the hypothetical posits, and we will decide both versions. If Verizon, for reasons genuinely its own — neither requested, encouraged, nor expected by the government — pinged the phone and then volunteered what it found, that is a private search under *United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109 (1984), and the government's receipt is no search to the extent it does not exceed the private search's scope. But the hypothetical as framed fails that test: a carrier that *continues a program it began only at police request*, with every reason to expect police use of the product, is performing a continuation of the government-initiated task — and the established two-part agency inquiry (government knowledge or acquiescence, plus the private party's purpose of assisting law enforcement) is flunked on both prongs. So the line the question asks for: compliance ends and private conduct begins when the private actor's purpose and the government's involvement are both genuinely severed from the original command — new reason, no expectation, no wink. Momentum is not initiative; an echo of the government's request is still the government's voice.

### Q17. If we accept ring two, is there any live controversy left for ring three — or is the question presented now a request for an advisory opinion?

**Answer:** If the Court accepts ring two, it need not reach ring three, and we have never pretended otherwise — the rings are alternatives, not a package deal. Unnecessary, however, is not advisory: an Article III case exists (a capital judgment turning on suppression), the doctrine's validity is pressed by both sides, and a court choosing among dispositive rationales urged by the parties is performing ordinary adjudication; courts routinely decide cases on grounds broader than the narrowest available, and the breadth of the chosen ground does not convert the opinion into advice. But our candid position is this: ring two is a holding the Court can issue *only if* it first rejects neither the question's premises — and the Government contests ring two vigorously, arguing voluntary conveyance and business-records status. If the Court agrees with the Government there, ring three is not merely live but dispositive. The order of operations belongs to the Court; we built the case so that every path through it ends in reversal, and we will not feign disappointment at whichever path the Court walks.

### Q18. Dictate Part I's holding — one sentence lower courts will apply — and tell us whether it turns on duration, open-endedness, or government initiation.

**Answer:** The sentence: "When the government directs or compels the prospective monitoring of a cell phone's location — obtaining fixes generated at and because of its request, whether from a carrier or its own equipment — it conducts a Fourth Amendment search, because such monitoring invades the reasonable expectation of privacy in the whole of one's physical movements; the monitoring's duration and precision measure the intrusion's depth, not the Amendment's application." So, answering the trichotomy directly: *government initiation* and *prospectivity* define the category — they distinguish surveillance from the retrieval of existing records, which remains *Carpenter*'s historical-CSLI domain. *Duration and open-endedness are aggravators, not elements*: this case's days-long, no-end-date stream makes it easy, but the rule does not depend on it. We accept the consequence that this commits us to in Q22 — a single exigent ping is a search too — and we will pay that bill there, because a duration element would force the dosage line the bench rightly suspects cannot be drawn.

### Q19. Is a one-hour tower dump capturing a thousand bystanders' phones a search as to each of them?

**Answer:** Under the chronicle rule, yes as to each — and under current law, it is *Carpenter*'s expressly reserved question. We answer both layers. A tower dump is not our Part I (it is retrospective retrieval of existing registrations, not commanded prospective generation), so the agency theory does not reach it; its classification falls to ring three, where location records are protected as a category — and category, as we said in Part One Q2, is not diluted by the number of people swept in or the brevity of the slice. The administrability answer is that this regime substantially exists: law enforcement already routes geofence-style bulk location queries through warrants, and magistrates already police their particularity, scope, and minimization. The thousand bystanders are an argument *for* judicial authorization, not against the search's existence: breadth is what warrants exist to discipline. A doctrine under which acquiring one person's location is a search but acquiring a thousand people's locations is nothing would have the Fourth Amendment rewarding scale.

### Q20. A government cell-site simulator captures the same fixes with no third party at all — same result or different? If same, what did the third-party doctrine ever have to do with your case?

**Answer:** Same result: a search — the Justice Department itself has required warrants for cell-site simulators by policy since 2015, anticipating exactly this conclusion. And the follow-up is welcome, because it states our case's logic rather than refuting it. The third-party doctrine has to do with this case for one reason only: *the Government raised it as a shield*. Our Part I never depended on which antenna answered — "the search is the acquisition, not the geography," and not the corporate intermediary either. The doctrine enters because the Government says the carrier's involvement *changes* the answer — converts surveillance into unprotected business records. Rings two and three exist to take that shield away: ring two because nothing was conveyed, ring three because the shield is rotten. So the simulator hypothetical proves coherence, not irrelevance — it shows that on our theory the constitutional result is invariant to the intermediary, which is precisely what a sound rule should be, and precisely what the third-party doctrine fails: under the Government's view, the same fixes are a search from a simulator and a nullity from a fax to Verizon. The Constitution should not care which antenna answered. On our rule it does not; on theirs it is dispositive.

### Q21. Police license-plate-reader networks chronicle every car with no third party; does your rationale condemn those — and if "that's a different case," why isn't that the deferral you fault in the Government?

**Answer:** We will decide it, not defer it. A single ALPR camera recording a plate at one point is classic *Knotts* observation — no search. A networked, retained, queryable ALPR database that can reconstruct a particular vehicle's movements across a region over time implicates the same "whole of physical movements" interest *Carpenter* protects, and on our rationale, querying such a database to reconstruct a person's movements is a search. The line between those poles is aggregation into a chronicle — the point at which ambient observation becomes the functional equivalent of tracking — and yes, locating that point in particular systems is application work for lower courts. Why is that not the Government's deferral? Because the two deferrals differ in what is withheld. We state the principle (whole-of-movements; observation becomes search upon aggregation into a queryable chronicle) and defer its *application* to records not before the Court — which is how judging works. The Government defers the *principle itself*: its answer to every hypothetical is that Congress will attend to it eventually. Deciding this case with a rule that reaches the next one is narrowness; declining to have a rule is abdication.

### Q22. If an officer asks the carrier one question — "which tower, right now?" — in fresh pursuit, is that a search? If not, give us the dosage rule.

**Answer:** Yes, it is a search — and therefore no dosage rule is needed, which is the answer's whole point. We refuse the tempting escape (one ping is too trivial to count) because it would commit us to the line-drawing the question rightly treats as impossible: if one ping is nothing, what of two, ten, a day's worth? Our rule has no dosage because duration is not an element (Q18): government-directed prospective location acquisition is a search at the first fix. The fresh-pursuit officer is not thereby disarmed — he is *justified*: a warrantless search supported by genuine exigency is lawful, and fresh pursuit of a fleeing suspect is the exception's birthplace, *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967). That is the architecture our brief committed to and we maintain under fire: flexibility lives in the exigency doctrine, where it is bounded, fact-bound, and reviewable — not in the definition of "search," where flexibility is forever. The dispute was never about dosage; it is about whether the safety valve is a doctrine with limits or a definition without them.

### Q23. Is § 2702(b)(8) facially constitutional — may Congress authorize disclosure on an officer's uncorroborated certification, or must a neutral decisionmaker stand in every emergency loop, including the abducted child's?

**Answer:** Facially constitutional, yes — because in its legitimate applications it operationalizes the exigent-circumstances exception, and exigent searches have *never* required a neutral decisionmaker in the loop; the absence of the magistrate is the exception's defining feature, tolerated precisely because a genuine emergency leaves no time for one. So the abducted child's officer certifies, the carrier discloses, and the Constitution is satisfied — not because the form blessed it, but because the emergency did. The constitutional work is done by the *fact* of exigency, not the certification: where a real emergency exists, the statute channels lawful conduct; where none exists, the form is a nullity and shelters nothing — as here, where the parties stipulate none existed. What Congress may not do is what the decision below would let this form become: a mechanism by which the officer's *uncorroborated say-so substitutes for* the existence of an emergency, judicially unreviewable in practice. Facial validity, as-applied vigilance: the form is constitutional; faith in the form is not.

### Q24. If the pathway is facially valid and the defect was one officer's lie, why isn't this a Franks problem with a damages remedy — what work does the false certificate do beyond atmosphere?

**Answer:** Because the lie is not what made this a search; it is what destroyed every excuse for one. Run the counterfactual that isolates our claim: an *honest* Doakes, candidly certifying "no emergency — I have simply lost track of a man I suspect," obtains the same stream. On the Government's doctrine, that honest acquisition is *equally* warrant-free, because the third-party doctrine asks nothing about emergencies — it strips the records of protection outright. That is the doctrinal problem, and it exists with or without the lie. The false certificate then does three jobs of work, none atmospheric: (1) it eliminates statutory authorization, by stipulation; (2) it eliminates the good-faith exception, because *Leon* withholds it from the officer who falsifies the predicate for the very process he invokes, 468 U.S. at 923, *cf. Franks*; and (3) it supplies the empirical verdict on the Government's central submission — that statutory safeguards make the warrant requirement unnecessary. The safeguard here was a checkbox, and it was defeated by a pen. A *Franks*-plus-damages regime answers the lie; it does not answer the doctrine that made the lie sufficient.

### Q25. What duty does the Constitution place on Verizon — verification, time limits, audit? If "none, that's Congress's job," haven't you relocated the calibration argument you reject?

**Answer:** None — the Fourth Amendment constrains the government, not the carrier — and no, the calibration argument is not relocated, because our rule removes the need for carrier gatekeeping rather than reassigning it. Under the regime below, the carrier's compliance desk is the *only* checkpoint between an officer and a citizen's continuous whereabouts, so everything turns on what Verizon verifies — and the record shows what that checkpoint is worth. Under our rule, the checkpoint is the magistrate: the constitutional safeguard sits *before* the demand arrives at Verizon, and the carrier's residual role is the modest one it plays in every warrant regime — honor valid process, decline invalid process. Congress remains free to add verification duties, time limits, and audits on top, and should; that is calibration *above* the floor, which we have never rejected. Our objection was only ever to calibration *as* the floor — to protocols in place of the magistrate rather than around one. The question's symmetry is elegant but false: assigning the gate to a neutral judge is not the same argument as assigning it to the searched party's vendor.

### Q26. Officers truthfully open a stream for a missing hiker, found safe within the hour; the still-running stream catches an unrelated crime on day three. Suppressed — or does inception immunize in both directions, only ever against the citizen?

**Answer:** Suppressed — and the question deserves a direct answer because it tests whether our inception rule is a principle or a one-way ratchet. It is a principle, and it cuts against the government here: exigency justifies an acquisition only as far as the exigency runs. A warrantless search "must be strictly circumscribed by the exigencies which justify its initiation," *Mincey*, 437 U.S. at 393 (quoting *Terry*); Title III embodies the same axiom, requiring termination upon attainment of the objective, § 2518(5). So the hiker stream is lawful at inception and for its justified hour; when the hiker is found, the justification lapses, and continued receipt is an ongoing warrantless acquisition with no current justification — day three's catch is suppressed. Now observe the symmetry with October 8: inception fixes the lawfulness of the *act undertaken* — it neither launders a stream opened without justification (Doakes) nor perpetuates a stream whose justification has died (the hiker). In both directions, the government's authority is exactly coextensive with its justification, no more, no less, in both cases. The ratchet the question fears is the *Government's* rule, not ours: it is Respondent who argues that a stream, once flowing, may be redeemed by whatever later swims through it.

### Q27. Be candid: if the rangers' discovery, the trunk, and the historical maps all survive remand, has your client won anything beyond another round before the same sentence — and should that affect how broadly we write?

**Answer:** Candidly: if every item survives remand, Morgan's practical winnings are a constitutionally clean process and another judgment day — and we do not concede that is nothing, least of all in a capital case, where the difference between a verdict resting on tainted linkage evidence and one resting on proof lawfully obtained is the difference the Sixth and Fourth Amendments exist to mark. But the premise should not be inflated into a prediction: the evidence the Government itself says "cinched the case" — the maps placing Morgan at the scene at the hour, and the trunk that made him the Butcher in the jury's eyes — is precisely the evidence whose independent source is unproven and whose chain to the fax is short and direct (Part One, Q28–Q29). The rangers' body proves a murder; the tainted evidence proves *Morgan*. As for breadth: the scope of the Court's writing should follow the legal question's needs, not a handicapping of remand — this Court decides what the Fourth Amendment means and lets lower courts apply *Nix* and *Murray* to exhibits, which is the ordinary and proper division of labor. A rule of decision is not made narrower or broader by one defendant's odds.

### Q28. The record paints your client as the deadliest serial killer in history; why is this capital case the right vehicle for the broadest Fourth Amendment overruling in fifty years — and what about hard facts making bad law?

**Answer:** It is the right vehicle *because* the facts are hard — in both directions, which is what the worry overlooks. The third-party doctrine's defense has always been institutional: warrants are unnecessary because statutes, carriers, and professional discipline will police the gap. This record is that defense subjected to its most favorable possible test — the most wanted man in America, the most motivated police force, the gravest crimes — and the safeguards' performance was: a frozen-out detective with a personal grudge, a stipulated-false checkbox, and days of warrantless tracking that no statute, no carrier, and no supervisor interrupted. If the doctrine's promise fails *here*, where every incentive ran toward care, it fails everywhere. And the unsympathetic client is the tradition, not the anomaly: this Court's Fourth Amendment landmarks were won by bootleggers, drug dealers, and armed robbers — *Katz* was a gambler, *Kyllo* a grower, *Carpenter* a serial robber — because constitutional rules are tested where the temptation to shortcut is strongest. A rule that holds when the defendant is loathed is a rule; one that holds only for the sympathetic is sentiment. As for bad law in either direction: the Court's protection against that is the three-ring architecture we built — it can size the holding to its confidence, and every ring decides this case.

### Q29. Forced choice: a unanimous narrow opinion — search, no exigency, remand, Miller untouched — or a 5–4 overruling Miller with a dissent inviting reversal. Which for your client, and which for the Constitution?

**Answer:** For the client: the unanimous narrow opinion, without hesitation — it is complete relief with no asterisk, and counsel's duty runs to Morgan before doctrine. And, answering honestly, for the Constitution too — so the answers do not differ. A 5–4 overruling with an invitation to undo it is not a constitutional settlement; it is a pendulum wound for the next docket, and the citizens whose records hang on it deserve better than doctrine with a five-year warranty. A unanimous holding that warrantless real-time tracking is a search and that invented emergencies excuse nothing would establish, with the full Court's authority, the warrant baseline for the surveillance form that matters most going forward — and it would leave *Miller* to fall in a case where its rationale is squarely dispositive, by which time today's unanimous reasoning will have done quiet work on it. None of this retreats from our merits answer: asked whether *Miller* should be overruled, we say yes, for the reasons briefed. But the question posed a forced choice between dispositions, and an advocate who would trade his client's clean win and the Constitution's stability for the word "overruled" in a fragile opinion has confused the scoreboard with the game.

### Q30. Your replacement rule will be administered through protocols — magistrate checklists, compliance manuals, circuit classification. Why are your protocols constitutionally superior to Congress's?

**Answer:** Because ours have a magistrate in them, and that difference is the Fourth Amendment. The Founders' grievance was never protocol as such — the general warrant was itself a protocol, scrupulously administered — it was *whose judgment* the protocol channeled: the searching officer's own discretion, unchecked by any neutral arbiter before the intrusion. Every protocol the question lists on our side — the checklist, the compliance manual, the classification precedent — exists to implement a prior decision by a neutral and detached magistrate on a sworn showing. The protocol the SCA's emergency pathway placed at the center of this case channels the opposite: the officer's self-certification of his own emergency, audited by no one until the suppression hearing of the man he tracked. The two regimes were bench-tested against each other on this record. The magistrate regime was never invoked, because it would have required Doakes to tell a judicial officer the truth: that there was no emergency, only a man he had lost sight of. The self-certification regime was invoked, and it performed exactly as designed: it transmitted his checkbox at fax speed. *Riley* said the Founders did not fight a revolution for agency protocols; we would complete the thought — they fought it to put a neutral magistrate between the officer's suspicion and the citizen's life, and protocols are constitutional exactly insofar as they keep him there.
