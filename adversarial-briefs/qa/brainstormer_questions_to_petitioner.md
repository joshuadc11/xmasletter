# Questions from the Bench (Neutral) — To Petitioner
## Dexter Morgan v. United States, No. 850-418 — Q&A Phase

*Thirty questions from a neutral, probing bench. The parties' own interrogatories already drill the chronicle/ledger classification exercises and the suppression inventory; these questions sit one level up — the institutions that must administer Petitioner's rule, the day after Miller falls, the agency theory's reach, the forward edge of the search holding, and what this client actually wins.*

---

### I. Living With the Chronicle/Ledger Rule: The Institutions That Must Administer It

**Q1.** Under current law a customer ordinarily has no standing and receives no notice when the government subpoenas his bank; if those records are now his "papers," does your rule require customer notice and a customer's motion to quash for every third-party subpoena — and what happens to grand-jury secrecy?

**Q2.** What is the custodian's obligation when a subpoena for mixed records arrives — must the bank or carrier classify chronicle versus ledger at its own legal risk, and what does its compliance department do on the morning after we rule?

**Q3.** Is the chronicle/ledger determination ex ante — governing what process the government must use before it asks — or ex post, a suppression question? If ex ante, who adjudicates it before anyone has seen the records?

**Q4.** Does your rule reach only criminal process, or also civil investigative demands, congressional subpoenas, IRS third-party summonses, and state administrative subpoenas — and if all of them, describe the first five years of litigation in agencies that have never run a probable-cause proceeding.

**Q5.** Your answer to hard cases is "as they arrive, as *Carpenter* did" — but you indict *Carpenter*'s case-by-case trimming as a "slow-motion overruling." Why is incrementalism a vice in our hands and a virtue in yours?

**Q6.** If a court of appeals misclassifies a record category under your constitutional rule, only we can fix it; under the statutory regime you would displace, Congress can fix it in a session. Why is the less correctable allocation of error the better one?

### II. Stare Decisis, Retroactivity, and the Day After

**Q7.** If we overrule *Miller* today, does the new rule apply on collateral review under *Teague*, and do you agree that *Davis* good faith insulates every conviction built on pre-decision subpoenas — answer carefully, because tens of thousands of closed cases turn on it.

**Q8.** Most subpoena practice is state and local; what weight do we give the reliance of fifty states whose investigative codes assume *Miller*, none of whom are parties before us?

**Q9.** *Carpenter* promised a "narrow" decision that did not disturb *Miller*; if we overrule *Miller* eight years later, what is our narrowness assurance worth in the next case — and why should anyone believe the limiting language you propose for this opinion?

**Q10.** Which opinion do you want us to write: a *Katz* opinion overruling *Miller*'s expectation analysis, or a positive-law opinion grounding protection in § 2702's nondisclosure command? They protect different records, and you have briefed both — choose.

**Q11.** If Congress repealed § 2702 tomorrow, would your client's expectation of privacy survive on your positive-law theory? If not, haven't you tied the Fourth Amendment's content to the grace of the very legislature you tell us cannot be trusted with it?

**Q12.** Was *Miller* wrong the day it was decided, or wrong only once technology changed? If the latter, constitutional holdings expire on technological notice — who keeps that clock, and what else is currently expiring?

### III. The Agency Theory's Reach

**Q13.** Under your agency theory, is Google the government's instrument when it runs a geofence query at warrant command — is every business that searches its own systems in response to legal process "generating" records on government command?

**Q14.** CALEA compels carriers to maintain real-time intercept capability and to assist wiretaps; on your theory, has Congress conscripted every carrier as a standing government agent, and does a Title III intercept now involve two searches or one?

**Q15.** If Verizon was the government's instrument, is it a state actor — could Morgan sue Verizon under § 1983 or *Bivens*? Do you accept that holding for every carrier that complies with legal process?

**Q16.** Suppose Verizon, on its own initiative, kept pinging the phone after Doakes's request and volunteered the results — private search, or fruit of the original agency? What does your answer tell us about where compliance ends and agency begins?

**Q17.** Your ring two asks us to hold the doctrine inapplicable to commanded generation; your ring three asks us to overrule it. If we accept ring two, is there any live controversy left in this record on which to decide ring three — or is the question presented now a request for an advisory opinion?

### IV. The Search Holding's Forward Edge

**Q18.** Dictate Part I's holding — the single sentence lower courts will apply — and tell us whether it turns on duration, open-endedness, or government initiation, because each ingredient decides a different set of future cases.

**Q19.** *Carpenter* reserved tower dumps alongside real-time CSLI; under your rationale that the acquisition is the search, not the geography, is a one-hour tower dump capturing a thousand bystanders' phones a search as to each of them?

**Q20.** A government-operated cell-site simulator captures the same fixes with no third party in the chain at all — same result or different? If same, what did the third-party doctrine ever have to do with your case; if different, why does the Constitution care which antenna answered?

**Q21.** Police license-plate-reader networks chronicle every car's movements with no third party involved; does your "whole of physical movements" rationale condemn those too — and if your answer is "that's a different case," why isn't that the same deferral you fault the Government for?

**Q22.** If an officer asks the carrier one question — "which tower, right now?" — during a fresh pursuit, is that a search under your open-endedness theory? If not, you concede some warrantless real-time location is permissible and the dispute is about dosage — so give us the dosage rule.

### V. Exigency, the Emergency Pathway, and the Carrier's Role

**Q23.** Is § 2702(b)(8) constitutional on its face under your theory — may Congress ever authorize disclosure of current location on an officer's uncorroborated certification, or must a neutral decisionmaker stand in every emergency loop, including the abducted child's?

**Q24.** If the emergency pathway is facially valid and the defect here was one officer's lie, why isn't this a *Franks* problem with a damages remedy rather than an occasion to restructure third-party doctrine — what work does the false certificate do in your argument beyond atmosphere?

**Q25.** What duty does the Constitution place on Verizon when a facially complete emergency form arrives — verification, time limits, audit? If your answer is "none, that's Congress's job," haven't you just relocated the calibration argument you reject?

**Q26.** You insist exigency is measured at inception. If officers truthfully open an emergency stream for a missing hiker who is found safe within the hour, and the still-running stream catches an unrelated crime on day three, is that evidence suppressed — or does inception immunize in both directions, and only ever against the citizen?

### VI. Remedy, the Vehicle, and What Your Client Actually Wins

**Q27.** Be candid: if the rangers' discovery, the trunk, and the historical trial maps all survive on remand, has your client won anything beyond another round of litigation before the same sentence — and should that affect how broadly we write?

**Q28.** This is a capital case in which the record paints your client as the deadliest serial killer in American history; why is this the right vehicle for the broadest Fourth Amendment overruling in fifty years, and what do we say to the worry that hard facts are about to make bad law in whichever direction we rule?

**Q29.** Forced choice: a unanimous narrow opinion — search, no exigency, remand, *Miller* untouched — or a 5–4 opinion overruling *Miller* with a dissent inviting the next Court to undo it. Which do you take for your client, and which for the Constitution, if those answers differ?

**Q30.** Your brief says the Founders did not fight a revolution for agency protocols — but your replacement rule will be administered through protocols: magistrate checklists, carrier compliance manuals, circuit-by-circuit classification. Tell us, in the end, why your protocols are constitutionally superior to Congress's.
