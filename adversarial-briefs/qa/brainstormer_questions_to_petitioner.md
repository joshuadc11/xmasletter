# Bench Questions for Petitioner — Morgan v. United States (QP I)

*From the neutral brainstormer, sitting as a hot bench. Thirty questions, grouped thematically. These probe Petitioner's weakest commitments: the chronicle/ledger rule's seams, the gap between winning the judgment and winning the question presented, the internal tension among the three rings, and the institutional aftermath of overruling Miller.*

## A. The Chronicle/Ledger Rule's Administrability

**Q1.** Classify these five records in a row, counsel, with one sentence of reasoning each: a pharmacy's dispensing log, a utility's smart-meter readings, an Uber trip history, a library's circulation records, and a hotel registry — and tell us which of your answers a district judge could have predicted in advance.

**Q2.** You concede a discrete credit-card purchase record falls on the ledger side; is it your position that twelve months of the same customer's credit-card statements — which *Carpenter* would call a chronicle of travels, purchases, and associations — flips to the protected side, and if so, at what statement does the flip occur?

**Q3.** Your sorting criterion asks whether a record's "evidentiary significance lies in reconstructing the customer's conduct" — but isn't that determined by what the *government* wants the record for, so that the identical bank statement is a ledger when sought for the bank's fraud and a chronicle when sought for the customer's whereabouts? Does Fourth Amendment protection really turn on the prosecutor's theory of relevance?

**Q4.** *Miller*'s own bank records were subpoenaed precisely to reconstruct Mitchell Miller's conduct — his whiskey-distilling operation. Under your evidentiary criterion, doesn't *Miller* itself come out the other way, and if so, how can you tell us its outcome "could survive"?

**Q5.** Who applies your rule at the front end — the carrier deciding whether to comply with a subpoena, the magistrate, or a suppression court years later — and what does a custodian of mixed records do in the interim besides litigate every request?

## B. Stare Decisis and the Question Actually Granted

**Q6.** You ask us to "overrule the rationale, cabin the result." Name one case in which this Court has announced it was overruling a precedent while expressly preserving that precedent's holding on its facts — and if there is none, why isn't your Part III just *Carpenter* with a louder title?

**Q7.** *Carpenter* declined to disturb *Miller* and *Smith* eight years ago, after full briefing on the same criticisms you press today. What has changed since 2018 — not since 1976 — that constitutes the special justification *Ramos* and *Janus* require?

**Q8.** If we adopt your chronicle rule, does *Smith v. Maryland* survive? Dialed numbers are a chronicle of the customer's associations under any honest reading — so tell us plainly whether every pen-register order now requires a warrant.

**Q9.** What result under your rule for *California v. Greenwood*'s trash, *United States v. White*'s false friend, and a confidential informant's report of what you told him at dinner — and if the answer to the last is "no protection," why does disclosure to a human betray you but disclosure to a server protect you?

**Q10.** Your own war room concedes that your winning ground — Parts I, II, and IV — is precisely the ground on which we need not overrule *Miller*. If we agree with you on all three, isn't the disciplined disposition to answer the question presented *against* you and rule for you anyway — and would you take that judgment?

**Q11.** You say constitutional stare decisis is weakest because Congress cannot correct us; but Congress *did* act here — the SCA — and you simultaneously celebrate that statute as positive law confirming your client's expectation. Why isn't the lesson that the political branches are managing this field and we should let them?

**Q12.** Is it your position that there was *no* legitimate reliance on *Miller* — none — across fifty years of grand-jury practice, tax enforcement, and anti-money-laundering law, or only that the reliance is outweighed? If outweighed, by what, measured how?

## C. The Agency Theory and the War Between Your Rings

**Q13.** Your Part II argues the third-party doctrine cannot reach this case *on its own terms* because nothing was conveyed. If you are right, the doctrine is fully coherent and simply inapplicable — so why are you asking us to overrule a doctrine your own argument shows is doing no work here?

**Q14.** If Verizon's network passively logged Morgan's tower registrations every thirty minutes for its own engineering purposes, and Doakes had simply asked for the most recent entry, would your agency theory fail? If yes, hasn't the Fourth Amendment line collapsed into the carrier's internal data-retention architecture, which no customer knows or controls?

**Q15.** The Government says the *Knotts* beeper was government-installed — zero conveyance — and its monitoring still wasn't a search, so conveyance vel non cannot be dispositive. Your answer is that this "defends the wrong doctrine" and collapses into Part I. Concede, then, that if we read *Knotts* the Government's way on the search question, your agency theory adds nothing — yes or no?

**Q16.** Suppose a private security firm, with no government request, pings its employees' phones and later volunteers the data to police under *Coolidge*'s private-search doctrine. Search or no search — and if no, doesn't your "commanded generation" line make protection turn on who pressed the button rather than what was revealed?

**Q17.** You quote *Carpenter*: location data is shared "without any affirmative act beyond powering up." But Morgan was a forensic analyst who knew exactly how carrier location works and carried the phone anyway while fleeing a crime scene. Why isn't your voluntariness argument weakest on your own facts?

## D. Limiting Principles: Duration, Geography, and the Single Ping

**Q18.** Give us the number, counsel. Is one real-time ping to locate an abducted child's phone a Fourth Amendment search requiring a warrant — and if your answer is "exigency excuses it," you have conceded it *is* a search, so tell us whether the officer who pings once with no emergency needs a warrant.

**Q19.** If a single ping is a search, you have gone beyond *Carpenter*, which reserved even seven days of historical data as its floor; if a single ping is not, you need a duration line just as badly as the Government does. Which horn do you take, and where is your line?

**Q20.** Your "the search is the acquisition, not the geography" argument is a capability test — the technique *could* have reached a bedroom. *Karo* held the opposite: monitoring becomes a search when it *actually reveals* the interior of a protected space. Are you asking us to overrule *Karo*'s actual-revelation holding too, or to distinguish it — and how?

**Q21.** *Knotts* reserved "twenty-four hour surveillance of any citizen" — a societal dragnet. Your record is one suspect, targeted, in a multi-agency manhunt. Your brief answers that targeting "is no comfort to the one"; but isn't that rhetoric conceding that the *Knotts* reservation, fairly read, does not cover this case?

**Q22.** Under your rule, may an officer who watches a suspect drive away simply ask the carrier "which tower is the phone on right now?" — once, during active pursuit — without a warrant? If not, you have constitutionalized the radio dispatch; if so, explain why thirty minutes of staleness and a second request change the analysis.

**Q23.** If Congress tomorrow amended the SCA to require a *court order on specific and articulable facts* — but not probable cause — for real-time location fixes capped at 48 hours, would that statute be constitutional under your theory? If not, what exactly is left for Congress to calibrate?

## E. Severability, Inception, and the Shape of Exigency

**Q24.** Your inception rule has a mirror image: if Doakes had faxed a *truthful* emergency form on October 8 and the emergency had dissolved on October 9 while the fixes kept arriving, your logic says the whole stream stays lawful. Doesn't measuring everything at inception immunize exactly the open-ended streams you say the Fourth Amendment condemns?

**Q25.** Title III requires ongoing minimization of a lawfully initiated wiretap — the law routinely evaluates continuing surveillance interval by interval. Why should a location stream be a single frozen event at inception when the closest statutory analogue treats it as a continuing course of conduct?

**Q26.** Suppose Agent Lundy — not Doakes, and not frozen out — had telephoned Verizon at noon on October 10, after the rangers' discovery, and asked for the two fixes at issue. Lawful without a warrant? If yes, then the constitutional violation here consists of the government *not making a phone call it didn't need to make* — why is suppression the proportionate answer to that?

**Q27.** You say exigency excuses a warrantless *act* and there was no act on October 10. But receipt and *use* are acts: Doakes read the October 10 fixes and relayed them to the FBI. Why isn't the knowing operational use of an incoming stream a fresh governmental acquisition each time an officer acts on it?

## F. Remedy, Fruits, and the Institutional Aftermath

**Q28.** If we hold this was a search, announce no exigency, and remand — and on remand the rangers' independent discovery, the historical trial maps, and the trunk search all survive under *Murray* and *Nix* — what, concretely, has your client won, and what have we actually deterred?

**Q29.** Your *Leon*/*Franks* answer covers the officer who falsifies the certificate. What is the rule for the *carrier* — does Verizon, facing a facially valid emergency form, now bear constitutional risk for complying, and what do you say to the carrier that responds by refusing every emergency request until a court rules?

**Q30.** Last question, counsel: if we write your chronicle rule into the United States Reports, every congressional committee subpoena, state attorney general CID, and Title 26 third-party summons for consumer records becomes a Fourth Amendment event to be litigated record-type by record-type for a generation. Tell us why that regime is more administrable than the one you call unworkable — *Carpenter* plus case-by-case trimming — which you yourself invoke every time your rule meets a hard case.
