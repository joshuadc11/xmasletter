# Respondent's Answers

**Morgan v. United States, No. 850-418 (October Term 2026) — Q&A Phase**

*Submitted by the Office of the Solicitor General, counsel for Respondent United States.*

*A note on the Government's consistent framework, so that no answer below is read in isolation. (1) The October 8 certification was false when made, and the October 8–9 disclosures violated the Stored Communications Act — that is the Government's affirmative position, not a grudging assumption; the Fourth Amendment question and the statutory question are distinct, Virginia v. Moore, 553 U.S. 164 (2008). (2) No fix in this record was a Fourth Amendment search, because none revealed anything beyond present movements in public places, United States v. Knotts, 460 U.S. 276 (1983); United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705 (1984). (3) The Government accepts a convergence principle as the outer limit of that submission: a real-time stream becomes a search no later than the point at which its accumulated fixes would, if sought as historical records, constitute the archive Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), protects. (4) If a search occurred, the October 10 acquisitions were reasonable under exigent circumstances, and in all events suppression is not the remedy on this record — at most, a remand for fruits analysis. Each answer below applies that framework.*

## Part One: Answers to Petitioner's Questions

### Q1. Yes or no: did any emergency involving danger of death or serious bodily injury exist anywhere in this record on October 8, when Doakes signed and faxed the form?

**Answer:** No. The record discloses no danger of death or serious bodily injury known to anyone on October 8: no in-progress crime, no missing person, no specific threat — only a manhunt for an at-large killer and a detective who, "in frustration," had lost sight of the colleague he had been stalking. The Government has never argued otherwise, and its exigency submission begins and ends with October 10. The seriousness of the Bay Harbor investigation in the abstract does not supply exigency; this Court said exactly that in Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385, 393–94 (1978), and we accept it.

### Q2. Do you concede the certification was false at the moment it was made?

**Answer:** Yes. Doakes certified an emergency that did not exist, and the Government does not defend the certification. The concession costs us nothing we ever claimed, because no part of our case depends on October 8 being lawful: Part II argues the acquisitions revealed only public movements (a question independent of Doakes's honesty), Part III attaches to October 10, and Part IV explains why the falsehood is answered by damages, discipline, and — if a search is found — an ordinary fruits analysis rather than automatic reversal.

### Q3. State, with record citations, every objective fact known to law enforcement on October 8 that could support exigency at the moment of the warrantless acquisition.

**Answer:** There are none that meet the doctrine's standard, and the Government claims none. The complete inventory of what the record shows was known on October 8 is this: eighteen victims attributed to an at-large serial killer (Op., Background); the FBI's conclusion that the killer was likely a Miami Metro insider and its freeze-out of Miami Metro personnel (id.); and Doakes's loss of visual contact with Morgan after weeks of extrajudicial stalking (id.). The first two are gravity-of-the-offense facts, which Mincey holds cannot themselves create exigency; the third is not a law-enforcement fact at all. That is precisely why the brief defends only October 10 — the day the record changed: rangers interrupted a Butcher murder in progress, forensically matched to the Bay Harbor Eighteen, and the perpetrator had fled and was unlocated (Op., Background).

### Q4. Given the stipulation that the SCA emergency exception does not apply, what record evidence lets you argue a Fourth Amendment "emergency" that did not exist under § 2702(b)(8)'s identical danger-of-death standard?

**Answer:** The October 10 facts: an interrupted homicide matched to the Butcher's signature, a killer in flight, eighteen prior victims attesting to the cost of delay. The two standards are substantively similar; they diverge in *what they evaluate and when*. The statutory exception is keyed to the disclosure that was actually procured — it asks whether the provider could in good faith believe an emergency required disclosure upon receipt of Doakes's October 8 form. The stipulation that the exception "does not apply" is a stipulation about that October 8 request, and we lose nothing by honoring it fully: no emergency existed on October 8. But a stipulation about an October 8 certification cannot be read as a covenant that no emergency ever arose in this case, because the record itself refutes that reading — a murder was interrupted in progress on October 10. We are not standing in daylight between two danger-of-death standards. We are standing on two different days.

### Q5. If a neutral magistrate had been presented on October 8 with everything Doakes knew, would a warrant have issued — and on what probable cause, for what crime?

**Answer:** No, and the Government does not contend otherwise. The record shows that on October 8 Doakes had suspicion and obsession, not probable cause as to Morgan; we make no claim that any judicial officer would or should have authorized tracking that day. That is the point of our architecture: October 8 is indefensible on every theory — no warrant, no statutory emergency, no exigency — and the Government defends none of it. Our case is that October 8's indefensibility does not decide the constitutional character of what the fixes revealed, the reasonableness of the October 10 acquisitions after the world changed, or the admissibility of evidence whose suppression Congress withheld and the exclusionary rule does not require. (To the extent the question gestures at October 10, the lawful-paths point made in Part IV is addressed, with its factual limits conceded, in the answer to Q29.)

### Q6. Identify, with record citation, each affirmative act any officer took on October 10 to acquire location data, as distinct from receiving fixes already flowing under the October 8 authorization.

**Answer:** Candidly: the record shows no new communication to Verizon on October 10. The governmental acts that day were these: the government received and accessed the two material fixes (Everglades; service station ten miles west) (Op., Background); Doakes analyzed them, inferred a direction of travel, and relayed the information to Special Agent Lundy (id.); and the FBI acted on it. Our submission is that receipt and use of the information *is* the constitutionally significant acquisition — Carpenter itself framed the search as the Government "accessing" the records, 585 U.S. at 313, and a Katz search occurs when the government obtains information in which a protected expectation exists, not when paperwork is filed. Karo supplies the method: a single installation, with monitoring parsed episode by episode. If the Court concludes that receipt without a fresh request cannot bear that weight, the same October 10 facts govern at the remedial stage, where the question is deterrence, not act-counting. We have built the argument to survive either answer.

### Q7. Yes or no: between the October 8 fax and Morgan's arrest, did any officer transmit any new request, certification, renewal, or communication to Verizon?

**Answer:** No. The record reflects a single fax on October 8 and nothing thereafter. We do not dispute it, and our Part III is built on that record rather than around it: the question is whether reasonableness in continuous electronic surveillance attaches to the act of requesting or to each acquisition of information. See Q6, Q8.

### Q8. State the rule: is reasonableness measured at the moment the warrantless act is undertaken (McNeely), or whenever each datum arrives — and cite this Court's decision adopting the latter.

**Answer:** The rule is the one McNeely states, and we accept it without qualification: reasonableness is measured by the objective circumstances confronting the government when it undertakes the warrantless act. 569 U.S. 141, 149–51 (2013). The disputed question — which McNeely, a single blood draw, had no occasion to answer — is what counts as "the act" when surveillance is continuous: the request that opens a stream, or each acquisition of information through it. No decision of this Court adopts the per-arrival measure in terms, and we will not pretend one does. But none adopts Petitioner's single-event-at-inception rule for data streams either; both sides are arguing from method. The closest analogue is Karo, which took one beeper installation and disaggregated its monitoring — condemning the episodes that revealed the home's interior, leaving the public-movement episodes undisturbed, 468 U.S. at 714–18. If episodes can be separately searches or not searches, they can be separately reasonable or not. And if the Court disagrees and dates everything to October 8, our position is not that the evidence is admissible because the act was reasonable — it is that the remedy question then takes over. Part IV.

### Q9. Quote the language in Karo holding that each transmission is a discrete governmental act whose justification is separately assessed, as opposed to parsing what the device revealed.

**Answer:** There is no such language, and our brief quoted none — we cited Karo's method, and we will state the distinction the question correctly identifies rather than paper over it. Karo parsed what an already-installed device *revealed*, holding the home-interior monitoring a search and the public monitoring not. 468 U.S. at 714–18. Our submission is that the same disaggregation extends to *justification*, for a reason internal to Katz: a search occurs, if at all, when protected information is acquired, and Karo establishes that acquisition through a continuous device is divisible — some episodes searches, some not. If the unit of analysis is divisible for the threshold question, it is divisible for the reasonableness question; nothing in this Court's cases welds the episodes together for one inquiry and severs them for the other. We acknowledge this is an extension of Karo's logic, not its holding — which is why Part III carries an express fallback: if the stream is indivisible, the October 10 facts control the exclusionary balance instead.

### Q10. Under your episode-by-episode theory, were the October 8–9 fixes each separate unlawful searches — and why does no taint attach to the October 10 fixes arriving under the same unrescinded, falsely procured authorization?

**Answer:** On the assumption Part III makes — that a search occurred — yes: each October 8–9 fix was a separate, unjustified, unlawful acquisition, and the Government accepts every remedial consequence that follows for those fixes. As for taint: the October 10 fixes are not the *fruit* of the October 8–9 fixes — nothing learned from the earlier fixes led to, enabled, or shaped the later ones; there is no investigative chain between them. The taint question is therefore whether the October 10 fixes are fruit of the October 8 *fax*, and on that question the intervening, wholly independent event — the rangers' discovery of an in-progress murder — is exactly the kind of changed circumstance that attenuation and exigency doctrine recognize. We do not pretend the analysis is one-sided: the flagrancy of Doakes's conduct would weigh in Morgan's favor under Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), which is precisely why the brief's ceiling is a remand for fruits analysis under Murray and Nix, not a declaration of harmlessness from the podium.

### Q11. You invite the Court to "assume the October 8–9 stream was unlawful": unlawful under what source of law — given that you say no fix was a search and the SCA is the only nonconstitutional norm — and with what consequence?

**Answer:** Under the Stored Communications Act, affirmatively and not by assumption: § 2702(a) forbade Verizon's disclosures, the stipulated inapplicability of § 2702(b)(8) and (c)(4) removes the only exception anyone has invoked, and the disclosures therefore violated the statute from the first fix. That is the Government's actual position. The word "assume" in Part III does additional, arguendo work: it permits the Court to treat October 8–9 as *constitutionally* unlawful too, and the Part still holds. The consequences track the source. Statutory unlawfulness carries the remedies Congress provided — § 2707 civil damages, including punitive damages for willful violations — and not suppression, as the courts of appeals uniformly hold. Constitutional unlawfulness, if the Court finds a search, carries the exclusionary analysis of Part IV. There is no contradiction in saying a disclosure violated a statute without being a Fourth Amendment search; that is Virginia v. Moore's holding running in the ordinary direction.

### Q12. If exigency can attach to passively arriving fixes whenever an emergency later ripens, what limiting principle prevents an officer from opening a falsely certified stream and waiting for events to supply the justification?

**Answer:** Four interlocking limits, and together they leave the open-first officer with nothing. First, every pre-emergency acquisition remains unlawful — statutorily always, and suppressibly so if a search — so the waiting period yields no usable evidence. Second, exigency justifies only acquisitions contemporaneous with a genuine, specific, objectively verifiable emergency; it cannot be banked in advance or stockpiled afterward, and it expires with the emergency (see Part Two, Q15). Third, the post-emergency yield is only what a *truthful* emergency request made that day would lawfully have produced — the falsification purchases zero evidentiary advantage over honesty. Fourth, the falsifier's personal exposure (§ 2707 damages, including punitive; discipline; the flagrancy weight his conduct carries in any attenuation analysis) is unaffected by the later emergency. An officer who opens a stream first and waits acquires liability while waiting and nothing extra when the emergency comes. The incentive the question fears does not exist, because the scheme's payoff is identically available without the misconduct.

### Q13. Yes or no: was the authorization Doakes obtained — time frame "current," no end date — a single open-ended request, not a series of discrete daily requests?

**Answer:** Yes. It was one open-ended request, and the Government has never characterized it otherwise — the brief calls the "current" box a measure of "the request's sloppiness." Two things follow, neither helpful to Petitioner. The open-endedness is itself a statutory defect: § 2702(b)(8) contemplates disclosure tied to a specific emergency, and an undated stream on one checkbox is the statute abused, remediable under § 2707. But the Fourth Amendment does not take the paperwork as its unit of analysis; it judges what the Government acquired and what that acquisition revealed. A boundlessly drafted request that in fact yielded a few days of fixes, two of them material, both in public, is judged on that yield — just as an overbroad subpoena is judged, for constitutional purposes, by what was actually obtained and how.

### Q14. Yes or no: had Doakes obtained the identical several days of fixes as historical records on October 11, would Carpenter govern? If yes, state the principle making the same record protected when compiled yesterday but not in real time.

**Answer:** No — and the asymmetry the question is built to expose does not exist in our position. Carpenter held that accessing *seven days* of historical CSLI is a search and expressly declined to decide "whether there is a limited period for which the Government may obtain" historical CSLI without Fourth Amendment scrutiny. 585 U.S. at 310 n.3. A roughly three-day historical request on October 11 would fall within that reservation: an open question, not a Carpenter-governed search. And the Government accepts full symmetry in the other direction: a real-time stream becomes a search no later than the point at which its accumulation would constitute the protected historical archive. The direction of assembly changes nothing; the size and character of the resulting record is the constitutional variable, and this record — on either direction of approach — sits below any line this Court has drawn.

### Q15. By October 10, Doakes held days of accumulated movements — at what point does a running real-time stream become the archive you concede is protected?

**Answer:** At the convergence point just stated: a real-time stream becomes a Carpenter search no later than when its accumulated fixes would, if requested as historical records, trigger Carpenter — on the only line this Court has drawn, seven days, with everything shorter reserved by footnote 3. By October 10, Doakes held under three days of thirty-minute-interval fixes — short of the seven-day archive Carpenter condemned and inside the reservation, whichever way the record is approached. We add one honest qualification: footnote 3 reserved rather than blessed the sub-seven-day zone, so the Court could draw a shorter line; our submission is only that wherever the line falls, two material fixes across three days, in open wetlands and at a public service station, do not amount to the "detailed, encyclopedic" chronicle that was Carpenter's concern.

### Q16. State the maximum duration of warrantless, open-ended real-time tracking your theory permits — a week, a month, indefinitely — and the doctrinal source of the limit.

**Answer:** Not indefinite, not a month, and not more than Carpenter's own line. The theory carries two independent ceilings. Durationally: the convergence principle caps warrantless real-time acquisition at, at most, the historical line — seven days on Carpenter's holding, and potentially less, since footnote 3 leaves the shorter zone open for this Court to close. Source: Carpenter itself, 585 U.S. at 310 n.3, 313. Spatially: duration aside, the moment any fix reveals information about the interior of a home or other protected space, that fix is a search. Source: Karo, 468 U.S. at 714–16. The Government does not ask the Court to bless any period beyond this record's roughly three days; it asks the Court to hold that this acquisition, on these facts, fell on the lawful side of both ceilings.

### Q17. Name one federal appellate decision holding that a multi-day, open-ended real-time stream — as opposed to hours-long fugitive locates — is not a search.

**Answer:** We cannot, and we will not inflate Hammond or Riley into one — our brief volunteered that those locates ran hours while this one ran days. But the ledger is empty on both sides: Petitioner can name no federal appellate decision holding a multi-day real-time stream *is* a search, either. Where duration-matched authority runs out, principle governs, and the principles are the ones we have offered: Knotts and Karo for what individual fixes reveal, and the convergence point for when accumulation changes the analysis. Hammond and Riley matter not as duration precedents but for their reasoning — that the rule for locating a suspect's present public position comes from Knotts, not Carpenter — and that reasoning does not expire at sundown of the first day.

### Q18. Riley involved GPS-grade data, yet you insist this record shows quarter-mile tower data: which precision characterization does the United States adopt, and does it change which precedents govern?

**Answer:** The United States adopts the tower-scale characterization: the only quantified accuracy anywhere in this record is the trial expert's "within a quarter mile," and "GPS data" appears only as the label in Morgan's own suppression motion, not as a finding — the carrier provided "cell-site data." And no, the answer does not change the governing precedents, which is why we can give it without hedging. Knotts and Karo turn on what the surveillance *revealed* — public movements versus protected interiors — not on the instrument's granularity; Riley's significance is that the Sixth Circuit found no search even for GPS-grade precision, so if the Court reads this record as more precise than quarter-mile, the case law we rely on already covers that scenario. Precision would matter if a finer fix had placed the phone inside a protected space. None did, at any granularity.

### Q19. Is a wiretap or warrant likewise judged solely by what it happened to capture rather than by the scope of its authorization — cite your authority.

**Answer:** No — and the difference is doctrinal, not convenient. Warrants and Title III orders are judged ex ante by their authorization because particularity is a constitutional requirement *of warrants*: an overbroad authorization fails on its face. Berger v. New York, 388 U.S. 41, 58–60 (1967). But where the claim is that *warrantless* surveillance was a search under Katz, the threshold question is what the government actually acquired and what it revealed — that is Karo's holding: the beeper's monitoring was a search insofar as it revealed the residence's interior, and not otherwise, 468 U.S. at 714–16. Doakes's fax was not legal process of any kind; there is no authorization to judge, only acquisitions. Its open-ended scope is a genuine defect — a statutory one, remediable under § 2707 — but it cannot transform fixes that revealed public movements into searches of something never searched.

### Q20. Yes or no: if the overnight October 9 fixes had placed Morgan's phone inside his home, were those fixes searches — and could Doakes know in advance which fixes in his stream would be lawful?

**Answer:** Yes to the first, no to the second, and we accept both. Any fix that actually revealed the phone's presence inside a home or other protected space would have been a search under Karo — full stop. And no, Doakes could not know in advance which fixes would cross that line; neither could the agents in Karo, who monitored a beeper that traveled where it traveled. That is what it means to proceed without a warrant: the officer bears the risk that what is revealed will be protected, and the constitutional consequence attaches to the fixes that cross the line. We readily concede the practical moral of that risk allocation — a prudent officer obtains a warrant, and most do. But ex ante uncertainty does not retroactively convert the non-revealing fixes into searches; Karo itself condemned only the monitoring that touched the home and left the rest standing.

### Q21. Do you concede a genuine abduction satisfies the exigency exception — and if so, what work does your no-search holding do that exigency does not already do?

**Answer:** Yes, conceded: a genuine abduction is a paradigm exigency, and a warrant rule with an exigency exception would still permit locating the child's phone. The no-search holding does three things exigency cannot. First, it covers the real and common middle band where an emergency is suspected but not yet establishable — the missing adult, the unconfirmed welfare check, the wanted fugitive whose dangerousness is debatable (Riley's and Hammond's actual facts) — where officers acting under an exigency-only regime act at peril of suppression. Second, it answers a different doctrinal question: exigency is an *excuse* for a search; Knotts holds that briefly locating a suspect's present public position was never a search needing excuse, and collapsing that distinction would unsettle every public-tailing case. Third, it keeps the constitutional event where it belongs — on what was revealed — rather than making every locate presumptively unconstitutional pending after-the-fact justification litigation. The brief's abduction line was an overbreadth point about Petitioner's rule, not a claim that abductions would go unaddressed; we are content to rest it on these three.

### Q22. If the third-party doctrine does no independent work on these facts, what is the Court's warrant for reaffirming Miller here rather than reserving the question?

**Answer:** The order granting certiorari. The Court directed the parties to address "[w]hether this Court should overturn Miller and invalidate the third-party doctrine"; Petitioner pressed it; the question will be answered. What the Government asks for under the label "reaffirm" is precise and modest: hold that Petitioner has not carried the special-justification burden and *decline to overrule* — a refusal, not a fresh, expansive endorsement, and certainly not new dicta extending the doctrine. We agree with the premise of the question more than Petitioner will like: because Knotts independently resolves the search question, this case is a poor vehicle for remaking third-party law in either direction, and the Court should say no more about Miller than the question presented requires. A disposition that denies the overruling request while resting the judgment on Knotts and the alternative grounds is exactly the opinion we seek.

### Q23. Yes or no: do you contend Morgan "voluntarily conveyed" the responsive fixes to Verizon — and if yes, identify his affirmative act of conveyance.

**Answer:** No. The Government does not contend that Morgan voluntarily conveyed the responsive fixes in the sense Miller and Smith use that term, and Carpenter's reasoning — that CSLI is generated "without any affirmative act on the part of the user beyond powering up," 585 U.S. at 315 — is why our brief expressly demoted voluntariness to a secondary theory and rested on Knotts. The concession is cabined, and deliberately: it is a concession about *this category of data*, which Carpenter already made for us eight years ago, not about the doctrine. Bank customers still write checks; callers still dial numbers; customers still hand businesses the records of their dealings. Miller governs what customers actually convey. This case does not require pretending location fixes are among those things, and we do not.

### Q24. Carpenter holds CSLI is not "shared" in any meaningful sense; explain how prospective fixes the user cannot perceive, refuse, or avoid are more voluntarily conveyed than the historical CSLI Carpenter protected.

**Answer:** They are not, and the Government does not argue they are. We accept Carpenter's voluntariness analysis for cell-phone location data and do not ask the Court to retract a word of it. Our submission rests on the other half of Katz: not whether Morgan shared the information, but what the Government's acquisition *revealed* — and what it revealed was a phone in open wetlands and at a public service station, movements exposed to anyone, which Knotts holds carry no reasonable expectation of privacy regardless of how the Government came by them. The Knotts beeper itself involved no conveyance whatsoever — the government installed it — and monitoring it on public roads still was not a search. Voluntariness is where Petitioner wants the case decided because it is where his facts are strongest; it is simply not the ground our case stands on.

### Q25. State the rule that tells the next court whether a third-party record falls on the Miller side or the Carpenter side — and apply it to a carrier's prospective location stream.

**Answer:** The rule is the one Carpenter itself announced and lower courts have administered for eight years: a third-party record falls on the protected side when it (1) reveals a deep, comprehensive chronicle of the customer's life — the "whole of his physical movements" or its equivalent in intimacy and reach; (2) is generated automatically and involuntarily, as an unavoidable incident of participation in modern life rather than by meaningful choice; and (3) gives the government a retrospective, effortless, population-wide capacity that conventional surveillance never had. 585 U.S. at 309–15. Records lacking those features — a business's ordinary records of its bilateral dealings with a customer — remain on the Miller/Smith side. Applied to a prospective stream: each fix used to locate a suspect's present public position is governed by Knotts/Karo (revelation, not conveyance), and the stream as a whole crosses to the Carpenter side at the convergence point — when its accumulation matches the protected archive. That is a rule a magistrate can apply, and is applying, today.

### Q26. Does your "nearly every record is both at once" objection to the chronicle/ledger rule apply equally to Carpenter's own line between protected CSLI and unprotected "ordinary business records"?

**Answer:** No, because the two lines sort by different variables, and only one of them is double-counted by every record. Petitioner's rule classifies by the record's *character or significance* — whose story it tells, the business's or the customer's — and that test fails because nearly every consumer record genuinely tells both at once; the classifier has no tiebreaker. Carpenter classifies by what the record *reveals in aggregate*: depth, breadth, comprehensiveness, and involuntariness of generation. Those are scalar, evidence-based questions with answers — seven days of everywhere-you-went differs measurably from one cleared check — and a record that is "both a ledger and a chronicle" poses no difficulty, because the test never asks which it really is; it asks how much of a life the government obtains by acquiring it. Line-drawing at the margins persists under any test, including Petitioner's. The difference is that Carpenter's margins are factual and narrow; the chronicle/ledger rule's margin is the entire field.

### Q27. Yes or no: do you concede § 2708's exclusivity clause is limited to "nonconstitutional violations" and supplies no answer to suppression if this Court holds a Fourth Amendment search occurred?

**Answer:** Yes. Section 2708 says "nonconstitutional violations," and the Government reads it as it is written: if this Court holds a Fourth Amendment search occurred, § 2708 does not block suppression. That is exactly how the brief deploys the provision — conditionally, against Petitioner's own positive-law theory: insofar as he locates the privacy interest in § 2702's disclosure ban (his Gorsuch-inflected move), the claim is statutory and § 2708 confines him to Congress's remedies; insofar as the claim is constitutional, the argument hands off to Herring and Davis, where the exclusionary rule's own cost-benefit limits — not the statute — do the work. We claim nothing from § 2708 beyond its text, and the concession leaves Part IV's three grounds standing exactly as briefed.

### Q28. Identify this Court's decision extending Leon/Davis good-faith treatment to an officer who himself falsified the predicate for the warrantless process he invoked.

**Answer:** There is none, and the Government has never cited one — Part IV does not invoke the good-faith exception at all, for precisely this reason. Leon itself withholds good faith where the affiant's falsehood procured the process, 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984); cf. Franks v. Delaware, 438 U.S. 154 (1978), and Doakes's certification would fall squarely within that carve-out if we were foolish enough to claim its shelter. Our remedial grounds need no one to have acted in good faith: Congress's choice of damages over suppression for statutory violations; the Herring/Davis cost-benefit balance as applied to the *specific evidence sought to be suppressed* (the October 10 fixes, by which time lawful access existed); and independent source as to the rangers' discovery. The absence of a falsifier's-good-faith case is a hole in an argument we deliberately never made.

### Q29. Is your "police could have obtained the same data lawfully within minutes" deterrence argument anything other than inevitable discovery or independent source — and if it is such a claim, do its factual predicates require proof on remand under Nix and Murray?

**Answer:** It is partly both things, and we concede the consequence the question demands. Insofar as the lawful-paths point is offered to defeat suppression of *particular exhibits* — to show specific evidence would have been or was acquired untainted — it sounds in inevitable discovery and independent source, and Nix and Murray require its factual predicates (probable cause as of October 10, the genuine availability of a telephonic warrant or truthful certification, the rangers' independence) to be found below, not assumed at this lectern. That is why the brief's stated ceiling is "at the very most . . . a remand for fruits-and-harmlessness analysis." But the point also does distinct, purely legal work in the Herring/Davis balance: the marginal deterrent value of excluding evidence is assessed in light of what lawful avenues existed, and that weighing is for this Court. We claim the legal point here and accept that the factual one belongs on remand.

### Q30. Cite the record evidence that the trial maps rested on a "historical" dataset distinct from the real-time stream, state when and under what process it was obtained, and explain why it is not the fruit of an arrest produced solely by the tracking.

**Answer:** The record evidence is the trial description itself: Agent Scuteri mapped "cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier" showing the phone "within a quarter mile" of the murder scene "around the time during which the murder happened" — a retrospective reconstruction of past position, which is the function of historical cell-site records, not of a thirty-minute-delayed live stream used to find a man. On the rest, candor: the record does not disclose when or under what legal process the trial dataset was obtained, and the Government will not invent a chain of custody. Nor do we deny the sequence Petitioner presses — the arrest the tracking produced is part of the causal history of the prosecution. Whether the trial maps are nonetheless admissible turns on independent source, inevitable discovery, and attenuation as applied to a dataset obtainable by ordinary process from the carrier at any time, against the backdrop of a crime scene the rangers found before and independent of any fix. Those are remand questions, and the brief asks for nothing more than that: if a search is found and taint is pressed, the disposition is a fruits remand — not suppression by appellate assumption, and certainly not the overruling of Miller.

## Part Two: Answers to the Brainstormer's Questions

### Q1. Dictate the holding that affirms you — the sentence lower courts will apply — and say whether "short-term, targeted" is measured in hours, fixes, or suspects, since the next case will be six days.

**Answer:** The sentence: *"The government's acquisition from a wireless carrier of discrete, prospective location fixes, sought to locate an identified suspect's present position, is not a Fourth Amendment search where no fix reveals information about the interior of a constitutionally protected space, see Karo, and the accumulated fixes do not amount to the comprehensive record of movements protected by Carpenter — at minimum, the seven-day archive Carpenter held protected in historical form."* The measure is neither hours nor suspects but *accumulated coverage* — the duration and density of the record the government ends up holding — because that is the variable Carpenter made constitutional. "Targeted" (one identified suspect, on a specific request) is a necessary condition, answering Knotts's dragnet reservation, but it is not the metric. So decided, the six-day case answers itself: six days of accumulating fixes presses against the seven-day line and a court applying the convergence principle may well find it crosses; this case, at roughly three days with two material public fixes, does not. We would rather give the Court a line that might catch the next case than a slogan that catches none.

### Q2. If the harm is the retrospective archive, a real-time stream becomes a search when its accumulated fixes would, requested historically, trigger Carpenter. Do you accept that convergence point?

**Answer:** Yes — fully, and it is load-bearing throughout these answers. The same archive does not change constitutional character with the direction from which it was assembled; once the government holds the encyclopedic record, it matters not whether it arrived in one production or in thirty-minute increments. We accept the convergence point because it is the honest entailment of our own theory: if Carpenter's mechanism of harm is the archive, then an archive assembled prospectively is equally a search when complete. The acceptance also disciplines our position against the open-ended slippery slope: it supplies the answer to the thirty-day hypothetical (Q6), caps the doctrine Petitioner says is limitless, and costs us nothing on this record, where under three days of fixes fall short of the only archive line this Court has drawn and inside footnote 3's reservation either way.

### Q3. What result for a tower dump under your rationale — thousands of bystanders, one moment, public places, a search as to none?

**Answer:** Under the rationale we press, a single-moment tower dump is not a search as to any individual: it reveals one datum per person — presence near one tower at one time, typically in public — and chronicles no one's movements; it is the antithesis of the archive. We will not disguise the discomfort in that answer, so we state its limits plainly. The dump implicates the *other* concern Knotts reserved — indiscriminateness — and that concern grows with repetition: serial or wide-area dumps that begin to assemble movement profiles of unsuspected persons cross toward Carpenter, and compelled productions of that breadth are independently policed by the reasonableness limits on compulsory process, Oklahoma Press Publishing Co. v. Walling, 327 U.S. 186, 208 (1946), and by the SCA's process requirements. Carpenter reserved tower dumps, 585 U.S. at 316; this case does not require the Court to resolve them, but if pressed, that is the Government's answer: one moment, no search; an accumulating mosaic of strangers, a different case.

### Q4. An FBI cell-site simulator captures the same fixes with no carrier in the chain — search or not? If your answer differs, the third-party doctrine is doing all the work you assign to Knotts.

**Answer:** The answer does not differ, which is the proof the question seeks: if an FBI-operated simulator captured a handful of fixes over a short period, locating one identified suspect's present position in public and revealing nothing about any protected interior, that is not a search — for exactly the reasons this case is not. Knotts is doing the work; the carrier's presence in the chain is doing none. (We note for completeness that simulators raise distinct questions our facts do not — they cause the handset to transmit to government equipment, inviting Jones-style trespass-on-an-effect arguments, and Department policy requires a warrant for their use as a matter of practice — but on the Katz question asked, the answer is the same.) We accept the implication cheerfully rather than resist it: the third-party doctrine is not load-bearing for this judgment, which is among the best reasons not to use this case to demolish it. Overruling Miller here would change nothing about this record. That is the definition of an advisory overruling.

### Q5. Carriers can generate AI-assembled "pattern of life" summaries from the same tower data; is a request for the summary a search while a request for the underlying real-time fixes is not — and what sense does that make?

**Answer:** Yes, the summary is a search, and the distinction makes exactly the sense Carpenter made. A pattern-of-life summary is the Carpenter archive in digest form — its entire function is to deliver the "familial, political, professional, religious" mosaic that the Court held protected; that the carrier's software, rather than an FBI analyst, performs the aggregation cannot matter, or Carpenter could be evaded by outsourcing the analysis to the records custodian. The constitutional variable is what the government acquires: a comprehensive chronicle (search) versus a suspect's present position (Knotts). Two discrete fixes used to find a fleeing man reveal where he is; a pattern-of-life summary reveals who he is. A doctrine that treats those identically — in either direction — has stopped measuring the intrusion. Ours measures it, which is why the two requests come out differently.

### Q6. Would you make this same no-search argument for a thirty-day real-time stream confined to public places? If no, name the day; if yes, what remains of Carpenter's "whole of physical movements"?

**Answer:** No. A thirty-day stream is a search, public places notwithstanding — Carpenter's core holding is that the whole of one's physical movements is protected even though each individual movement is publicly exposed, and thirty days is the whole of one's movements four times over. The day: the convergence principle supplies it — no later than the day the accumulated stream matches the archive Carpenter protected, which on the only line this Court has drawn is day seven; footnote 3 leaves the Court free to set it earlier, and nothing in the Government's submission asks for a day beyond the roughly three this record contains. We decline only to pretend the Constitution specifies a clock-time the cases have not yet fixed; Carpenter itself drew its line at the facts before it ("It is sufficient for our purposes today"), and we propose the same judicial modesty, with the seven-day mark as the established ceiling.

### Q7. Is footnote 3's reservation a one-way ratchet — or do you concede it leaves us free to rule against you on real-time data without disturbing a single precedent?

**Answer:** Conceded, without hedging: the reservation is not a ratchet in anyone's favor. Carpenter's footnote 3 and its real-time reservation leave the Court entirely free to hold real-time tracking a search without overruling or even narrowing any precedent, and we have never argued otherwise. Our use of the reservation is purely defensive and singular: it refutes Petitioner's claim that Carpenter *already* decided real-time tracking a fortiori — a reservation of the question is inconsistent with the question having been answered. Beyond that, the slate is open, and the Government must win on reasons, not reservations: the revelation principle of Knotts and Karo, the absence of any archive, the public character of every material fix. And if the Court rules against us on the search question notwithstanding, the reservation's openness cuts our way once more — such a holding requires no disturbance of Miller, and the judgment still stands on Parts III and IV.

### Q8. You take the SCA's reliance benefit while resisting its expectation cost: § 2702 forbids disclosure, yet you say the customer "knowingly exposes" the data. Which position do you surrender?

**Answer:** We surrender "knowing exposure" — as to this category of data — and we surrendered it eight years ago when the United States lost Carpenter: cell-phone location data is not meaningfully shared or knowingly exposed, and no answer in this set claims otherwise (see Part One, Q23–Q24). What we retain is the reliance argument, which never depended on the premise surrendered. The legislative-reliance point is not that customers expect disclosure; it is that Congress designed an entire graduated-process architecture — § 2703's tiers, § 2702's ban and exceptions — on the settled constitutional baseline that compelled production of third-party business records is not a search, and wholesale overruling removes the foundation of that design. Indeed, § 2702's disclosure ban proves our structural point rather than contradicting it: Congress protecting privacy *above* the constitutional floor is the system working — statutory privacy where the Constitution is silent is a policy choice, not evidence that the constitutional floor was always somewhere else. Miller itself so treated the Bank Secrecy Act's regime.

### Q9. Does the Government accept Warshak? If yes, state the principle protecting content conveyed to a provider's servers while location generated to its towers is not.

**Answer:** Yes — the Government does not contest Warshak's warrant rule for email content, and Department practice has long conformed to it: warrants for content. United States v. Warshak, 631 F.3d 266 (6th Cir. 2010). The principle is the oldest line in this field: content versus non-content. The contents of communications have been protected in third-party hands since Ex parte Jackson, 96 U.S. 727 (1878) (sealed letters in the government's own mails), and Katz (words carried on a third party's wire); the routing, addressing, and operational data incident to delivering the service have not, Smith, 442 U.S. at 741–43. Email content is the letter; tower-registration data is the postmark. Location data sits on the non-content side of that line — though we acknowledge Carpenter holds that even non-content location data becomes protected at archive scale, which is why our position carries the convergence ceiling rather than resting on the content line alone.

### Q10. If Knotts decides the case, why ask us to reaffirm Miller rather than reserve it — and if you need Miller reaffirmed, defend voluntariness for a fix the customer never makes, never sees, and cannot refuse.

**Answer:** We do not need Miller reaffirmed to win, and we will not defend voluntariness for these fixes — both halves answered directly. The judgment stands on Knotts alone; voluntariness for carrier-generated fixes is indefensible after Carpenter and our brief expressly declines to rest on it. The reason we nonetheless ask the Court to "reaffirm" Miller is that the question presented requires an answer: certiorari was granted on whether to overrule, Petitioner has pressed it through three rounds, and the Court will say something about it. What we ask the Court to say is the minimum: Petitioner has not carried the special-justification burden, and the request to overrule is declined — a holding about stare decisis, not a fresh embrace of assumption-of-risk reasoning for the digital age. If the Court prefers to decline the overruling request while expressly reserving Miller's long-term fate, the United States wins that case too, and says so.

### Q11. Smith presumed callers know operators see dialed numbers; what must the 2026 user be presumed to know — and does your doctrine attribute to every American the knowledge of a telecommunications engineer?

**Answer:** No engineer's knowledge is required, because we do not extend Smith's presumption to location data at all — Carpenter rejected that extension and we accept it; nothing in our case charges Morgan with constructive knowledge of tower-registration protocols. For the categories where Miller and Smith still operate, the presumed knowledge is the ordinary commercial understanding Smith actually relied on: a customer knows the bank records his deposits because it sends him statements of them; a caller knows the company processes the number because the call connects and the bill itemizes it. That is folk knowledge of a bilateral transaction, not network engineering. Where genuine knowledge runs out — where data is generated invisibly, involuntarily, and pervasively — the doctrine runs out with it, and Carpenter is the Court's own statement of that limit. Our framework keeps Smith honest by keeping it small.

### Q12. If voluntariness is a fiction you no longer defend, the doctrine stands on one leg — knowing exposure — and Carpenter cut that leg for location data. What are we reaffirming, other than the word "Miller"?

**Answer:** A holding, with two legs that remain sound for the records it actually governs. First, the documents leg — Miller's actual core: the records sought are "the business records of the banks," not the customer's papers, 425 U.S. at 440–41; the Fourth Amendment protects the people's "persons, houses, papers, and effects," and a business's own records of its side of a transaction are its papers, not the customer's. Second, the diminished-expectation leg: in records of *bilateral commercial dealings* — transactions the customer genuinely and knowingly conducted with the business — the customer retains no reasonable expectation that the business's documentation is immune from compelled production. Both legs stand in 2026 for cleared checks, account ledgers, dialed numbers, and subscriber records. What Carpenter cut away is the doctrine's application to involuntary, automatically generated chronicles — and we reaffirm Miller *as so limited*, which is the only Miller anyone has defended since 2018. The reaffirmation preserves the categorical framework that decides tomorrow's grand-jury subpoena without a suppression hearing; that is not a word, it is the operating system of compulsory process.

### Q13. Draft the exigency paragraph of our opinion so it does not teach officers to open streams first and await emergencies. Can it be done — or is the warrant the cheaper teacher?

**Answer:** It can be done, and here is the paragraph: *"Exigent circumstances justify the warrantless acquisition of location information only where, at the time of each acquisition, officers have an objectively reasonable basis to believe that locating the person is necessary to prevent death or serious injury or to apprehend a dangerous suspect in flight. The exigency justifies only acquisitions contemporaneous with it: information acquired before the emergency arose is not retroactively excused and remains fully unlawful and remediable, and acquisitions after the emergency ends are likewise unjustified. An officer who initiates surveillance in advance of any emergency therefore gains nothing his lawful options would not supply — the pre-emergency yield is unusable and actionable, and the post-emergency yield is no more than a truthful contemporaneous request would have produced."* That paragraph teaches the open-first officer that his scheme is all cost and no benefit. We concede the kernel of truth in the question's last clause: the warrant is often the cheaper and better course, McNeely, 569 U.S. at 154–55, and nothing in our submission discourages it. But the front-line teacher in this statutory scheme is the carrier's gatekeeping under § 2702 and the falsifier's personal exposure — suppression litigation was never the system's only classroom.

### Q14. Were the October 8 and 9 fixes dozens of discrete Fourth Amendment violations under your episode theory — and why does the Government count its lawful episodes but never total its unlawful ones?

**Answer:** On the assumption that a search occurred: yes — every pre-exigency fix was a discrete, unjustified acquisition, and at thirty-minute intervals over two days that is dozens of violations, each independently remediable. We accept the totaling without flinching, because the symmetry the question demands is one our theory already entails: episodes count both ways or the theory is a fraud. Morgan would hold a claim for each unlawful acquisition — § 2707 damages on the statutory account, constitutional claims on the assumed one — and the aggregate flagrancy of the October 8–9 campaign weighs against the Government in any attenuation analysis (see Part One, Q10). What the Government resists is not the arithmetic but the cross-contamination: dozens of unlawful acquisitions on Days One and Two do not convert a separately justified acquisition on Day Three into their fruit, any more than Karo's unlawful in-home monitoring retroactively poisoned its lawful highway monitoring. Count them all; remedy them each; but remedy each for what it was.

### Q15. If exigency attached on October 10, did it expire at the drive-thru arrest? Was the stream still running after Morgan was in custody — and whose problem is that?

**Answer:** Yes, it expired at the arrest — exigency lives exactly as long as the emergency, and the emergency ended when Morgan was in custody. The record does not say whether fixes continued to arrive afterward under the never-rescinded certification; if they did, those acquisitions were unlawful — statutorily without question, and constitutionally on the search assumption — justified by nothing, and they are the Government's problem and Doakes's, not Morgan's evidentiary problem, since no post-arrest fix was offered or used at trial. We accept the question's implicit point rather than dodge it: the unrescinded, open-ended certification is a genuine defect of this statutory scheme as administered — disclosures should be time-limited and terminable — and the answer is the one we give throughout: § 2707 liability for the unjustified acquisitions, and statutory repair of the open-endedness, which Congress can accomplish tomorrow and a constitutional overruling never could.

### Q16. How many § 2702(b)(8) emergency requests do carriers receive yearly, and what fraction are audited or found false? If you cannot say, why calibrate doctrine to the assumption that Doakes is rare?

**Answer:** The record contains no such figures and the Government will not manufacture them at the lectern. What can be said responsibly: the major carriers' published transparency reports disclose that emergency requests number in the tens of thousands annually, and systematic post-hoc auditing is, so far as we are aware, sparse — a state of affairs we will not defend as adequate. But the inference the question builds on that candor runs backward. Our doctrine is not calibrated to Doakes being rare; none of our four grounds depends on the frequency of false certifications. The no-search holding turns on what was revealed, true for honest and dishonest requesters alike; the exigency holding turns on October 10's objective facts; the remedial holding turns on Congress's choice and the deterrence balance for this evidence. If false certifications are common, that is a powerful case for the legislative fixes this regime can absorb — mandatory judicial review within days, audit and reporting duties, termination requirements — and Congress's capacity to make exactly those repairs is the heart of our Part I.C, not a casualty of the concession.

### Q17. Should Verizon have done anything differently — or did the carrier behave exactly as the system intends when it tracked a man for days on one unverified checkbox?

**Answer:** Yes, Verizon should have done differently, and the Government will not pretend this is the system working as intended. Section 2702(b)(8) permits disclosure only when the provider, in good faith, believes an emergency requires it — a standard that presupposes the provider exercises judgment, not a rubber stamp; and whatever can be said of honoring an emergency form on day one, continuing an open-ended stream for days, without seeking particulars, confirmation, or an endpoint, is not the gatekeeping the statute contemplates. A provider's good-faith belief is not a one-time event; it should be re-examined as a disclosure runs on. We say this without embarrassment because it strengthens rather than weakens our architecture: the SCA's safeguard failed here at the carrier tier as well as the officer tier, and both failures are legislatively and administratively reparable — verification requirements, durational limits, automatic sunset of emergency disclosures. The remedy for a defeated safeguard is to fortify the safeguard, not to constitutionalize every business record in the economy.

### Q18. The danger-of-death standard appears in both the statute you stipulated away and the constitutional argument you now press; what daylight between the two identical standards are you standing in?

**Answer:** None — and that is precisely our answer. We do not contend that October 10's facts satisfy the Fourth Amendment while flunking the statute; on October 10 those facts would have satisfied *both* — a truthful emergency certification faxed that morning would have been statutorily valid. The stipulation does not say otherwise, because the stipulation is about the disclosure actually procured: Doakes's October 8 certification, measured by what the provider could in good faith believe upon receiving it, on a day when no emergency existed. We concede October 8 fails both standards too — statute and Constitution alike. So the Government is not standing in daylight between two standards; it is standing on the difference between two days, and the stipulation cannot be read to erase October 10 from the record without erasing the interrupted murder itself. The substantive standards are aligned, which is Congress's craftsmanship working: the statute's emergency pathway was built to mirror the constitutional exigency it codifies.

### Q19. Walk me to the end of your remedial road: no suppression, § 2707 damages against a judgment-proof officer behind qualified immunity, no Bivens after Egbert. Who concretely enforces the right you concede may exist?

**Answer:** We will walk it honestly, because the road is thinner than we would wish, though not empty. For the October 8–9 violations: a § 2707 action lies against the violator, with statutory minimum damages of $1,000, punitive damages for willful or intentional violations, and attorney's fees, § 2707(c) — and critically, the statute's good-faith defense, § 2707(e), is unavailable to an officer who *fabricated* the predicate, so Doakes does not hide behind it; add departmental discipline and potential state-law liability for a campaign of stalking. For the systemic level: carrier gatekeeping ex ante and congressional repair (Q16–Q17). We concede what cannot be denied — after Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), constitutional damages against the federal agents are unlikely, and civil enforcement against individual officers is hard everywhere. But note what suppression would and would not add: the exclusionary rule protects only the tracked citizen who is *criminally charged with something to suppress*. The person this regime most needs to protect — the innocent target tracked by a vindictive officer and never charged — gets nothing from exclusion and everything from § 2707. If the remedies are too weak, the answer is to strengthen the remedy Congress built for everyone, not to enrich the one remedy that serves only defendants.

### Q20. Name one false-certification case — one — in which an officer was prosecuted, disciplined, or held liable under § 2707. If silence, why should doctrine rest on a hypothesis?

**Answer:** We cannot name one, and we will not dress the silence up. No reported decision known to the Government imposes § 2707 liability for a false emergency certification, and we will not claim unreported settlements or discipline we cannot document. Two things follow from that concession, and one does not. What follows: the enforcement record of the statutory remedy is unproven, and the Court should weigh our "other means to punish" argument at that discounted value; and Congress has work to do (Q16). What does not follow: that suppression becomes the remedy by default. The exclusionary rule is not a gap-filler awarded whenever civil remedies underperform — its application turns on the deterrence-versus-cost balance for the specific evidence in the specific case, Herring, 555 U.S. at 141, and on this record the evidence sought to be suppressed is the October 10 yield, as to which lawful access existed and exclusion deters nothing (Part One, Q29 states the limits of that point). The thinness of § 2707's track record is a true fact about the world that argues for statutory reinforcement; it is not an argument that suppressing this evidence in this case would teach anyone anything.

### Q21. If we hold this was a search but deny suppression, we announce that warrantless real-time tracking violates the Constitution and costs the government nothing. What does that opinion teach the next Doakes?

**Answer:** More than the question allows — because "costs nothing" misdescribes the opinion's content. The opinion the question posits would hold: (1) warrantless real-time tracking is a Fourth Amendment search, which transforms primary conduct immediately and everywhere — carriers, whose lawyers read this Court's opinions, will demand warrants or genuine emergencies before disclosing, closing the fax-form pathway prospectively for everyone, including officers never deterred by suppression; (2) pre-emergency acquisitions are unconstitutional, so the next Doakes's October 8–9 yield is suppressible in any case where it matters and actionable in every case; and (3) suppression was denied *here* only because the material evidence coincided with a genuine emergency and an independently discovered crime scene. The next Doakes learns that falsification buys him liability plus nothing — not that tracking is free. We add the candid coda: if the Court is persuaded a search occurred and finds the deterrence case stronger than we do, the disposition our brief itself names is a fruits remand. Even our defeat on this point is structured to cost the public no more than it must.

### Q22. Under Herring's own culpability framework, isn't deliberate falsification the core case for exclusion rather than the exception to it?

**Answer:** Yes — as to culpability, we concede it outright. Herring's scale runs from negligence to "deliberate, reckless, or grossly negligent conduct," 555 U.S. at 144, and a fabricated emergency certification sits at the top of it; if the evidence at issue were the direct yield of that falsification and nothing else, the deterrence case for exclusion would be at its strongest, and the Government would not be standing here pretending otherwise. The cabin: culpability is one side of Herring's ledger, not the whole of it. Exclusion still requires that the evidence suppressed be the fruit of the culpable act and that suppressing it yield deterrence worth its cost — and here the evidentially material acquisitions arrived when an objective emergency independently justified them, the crime scene was found by rangers before and apart from any fix, and the conviction's remaining pillars stand at most one attenuated step from the misconduct. Deliberate falsification makes this the core case for excluding the *October 8–9 fruits*; it does not make it a case for suppressing evidence the falsification did not earn. And our primary position stands in front of all of this: no search occurred, and the exclusionary rule never engages.

### Q23. Is the Government's true position that the exclusionary rule should never apply to carrier-disclosure violations, constitutional or otherwise? Say it plainly.

**Answer:** Plainly, then, in two sentences. For *statutory* violations of the SCA — disclosures that violate § 2702 but effect no Fourth Amendment search — the exclusionary rule never applies, because Congress chose damages and omitted suppression, and the courts of appeals have uniformly respected that choice; that is a position about congressional remedial design, and we hold it categorically. For *constitutional* violations — where a carrier disclosure is or enables a Fourth Amendment search — the exclusionary rule applies under its ordinary Herring/Davis framework, and the Government claims no carrier-context exemption from it; if a court finds a search, flagrant misconduct, and fruit, suppression follows as it would anywhere else. Congress's silence on suppression was a choice about statutory violations; it neither could nor did purport to limit the judge-made remedy for constitutional ones, and our § 2708 concession (Part One, Q27) says exactly that.

### Q24. Yes or no: is Miller's reasoning — disclosure to anyone, for any purpose, forfeits protection against everyone — correct on its own terms in 2026? If you cannot say yes, you ask us to retain a precedent on reliance alone.

**Answer:** Yes as to Miller's holding and its operative rationale; no as to the maximal dictum the question quotes — and the distinction defeats the trap rather than springing it. What Miller held, and what remains correct in 2026: a business's own records of transactions a customer knowingly conducted with it are the business's papers, and the customer has no reasonable expectation that they are immune from compelled production directed at the business. 425 U.S. at 440–43. That reasoning is sound today for the records it governs — cleared checks, account records, dialed numbers. What we do not defend is the slogan-level generalization — *any* disclosure, of *anything*, forfeits *everything* — but this Court already refused to take Miller at that altitude in Carpenter, and a precedent is its holding, not its broadest restatable dictum. So no, we are not asking for retention on reliance alone: we defend the holding as correct, the dictum as already and properly trimmed, and reliance as the additional reason the trimmed holding should not be uprooted. Retention of a sound-as-limited precedent is not grace; it is stare decisis doing its ordinary work.

### Q25. Statutes are written against moving constitutional floors, and Carpenter moved this one without collapse. Name the concrete enforcement program that fails if we hold only that government-commanded prospective tracking requires a warrant.

**Answer:** None — conceded. A holding confined to government-commanded prospective location tracking would not topple any enforcement program the Government can name: such locates are a small fraction of process volume, most already proceed by warrant or genuine emergency, and Carpenter proved that a targeted, category-specific constitutional adjustment is absorbable. We make that concession because it is true, and because it sharpens rather than surrenders our actual point: the question presented is not whether to make that narrow holding — it is whether to overrule Miller and invalidate the third-party doctrine, and *that* holding has no such containment. The wrecking ball threatens grand-jury subpoenas, tax and securities enforcement, and the entire subpoena economy; the scalpel threatens nothing, which is exactly why Carpenter used it. If the Court is inclined toward the narrow tracking holding, the Government loses little and says so candidly — and the judgment below is still affirmed under Parts III and IV. What we resist is letting the narrow holding be the vehicle for the broad demolition the question presented actually proposes.

### Q26. If Congress is the right calibrator, state the constitutional minimum below which Congress may not legislate — or is the answer zero?

**Answer:** Not zero. The floor exists and this Court's cases already state it; Congress calibrates above it, never below. The minimum, as the law stands: the contents of communications in third-party hands are protected (Ex parte Jackson; Katz; the principle Warshak applies); comprehensive chronicles of physical movement and their equivalents are protected regardless of third-party custody (Carpenter; the Jones concurrences); monitoring that reveals the interior of homes and protected spaces is a search however effected (Karo; Kyllo v. United States, 533 U.S. 27 (2001)); and even compelled production of unprotected records must satisfy the reasonableness limits on compulsory process — relevance, particularity, non-oppressiveness (Oklahoma Press). Below none of those may Congress legislate; statutory silence would not strip a citizen of any of them. What remains in the legislative zone is the calibration of process for ordinary business records — the zone where Miller governs — and assigning that band to democratic adjustment is not abandonment of three hundred million people; it is the allocation Carpenter itself performed when it drew the constitutional line and left the rest to the SCA.

### Q27. Petitioner's wrecking ball never swings if you concede that commanded prospective tracking is a search; the Government refuses even that. Why — what do you actually lose in the next ten years?

**Answer:** In candor, and consistent with Q25: not much, in the run of cases — most prospective locates are emergencies or warrant cases already, and the Government's institutional interests would survive the concession. We refuse it anyway, for three reasons that are doctrinal rather than self-interested. First, the concession would be wrong on these facts: it defines "search" by the *capability* of a technique rather than by what it revealed, and this Court has never taken that step — Karo condemned actual revelation of the home, not the beeper's capacity to enter one; adopting a capability test here would unsettle Knotts and every public-surveillance case built on it. Second, this is the wrong record for the rule: a case in which every material fix showed public movement asks the Court to announce a search holding with no search-like intrusion in it. Third, the concession is unnecessary to cabin the doctrine: the convergence principle and Karo's protected-space line already supply the limits, and Parts III and IV affirm the judgment even if the Court disagrees. If the Court concludes that commanded prospective tracking is a search, the Government loses this argument, accepts the warrant practice, and still prevails on this judgment — but it ought to lose the argument to a holding about what surveillance reveals, not a presumption about what it might have.

### Q28. Apply the doctrine you defend to this Term's technologies: Alexa audio, smart-meter occupancy inference, a wearable's biometric stream, an AI assistant's conversation logs, a consumer DNA database. Which does the Government concede are protected, and under what test, announced where?

**Answer:** We take each, deciding rather than deferring, under the framework already announced in this Court's cases (content — Jackson/Katz; home interiors — Kyllo/Karo; comprehensive involuntary chronicles — Carpenter; ordinary business records — Miller/Smith). **Alexa audio:** protected — it is the content of communications and conversations captured inside the home; warrant required, twice over (content rule plus the home). **Smart-meter occupancy inference:** protected as a search where granular enough to reveal in-home activity — that is Kyllo's principle applied to a utility's sensor, and the Seventh Circuit so held in Naperville Smart Meter Awareness v. City of Naperville, 900 F.3d 521 (7th Cir. 2018), while finding the particular collection reasonable; we accept both halves. **Wearable biometric stream:** protected — a continuous record of the body's functioning is a comprehensive involuntary chronicle on Carpenter's side of the line, with depth exceeding location data. **AI assistant conversation logs:** protected — content, full stop; the Warshak principle does not care whether the interlocutor is human. **Consumer DNA database:** the genetic profile itself is protected against compelled production as the deepest of chronicles — though we note identification-matching by lawfully obtained samples raises distinct questions not answered here. The pattern is the point: the Government concedes four and a half of five, under tests announced in existing cases — which is the demonstration that the framework Petitioner calls limitless already has, and enforces, limits.

### Q29. State the Government's operative test for when Miller governs and when Carpenter does — depth, breadth, comprehensiveness, voluntariness, something else — or concede the framework is a reservation wrapped in a narrowness promise.

**Answer:** The test, stated for the magistrate who must apply it tomorrow morning: a third-party record requires a warrant when **(1)** it consists of communications content (Jackson/Katz/Warshak), or **(2)** it reveals the interior of a home or protected space (Kyllo/Karo), or **(3)** it constitutes a deep, comprehensive chronicle of personal activity — judged by depth, breadth, and retrospective reach — generated automatically and involuntarily as an unavoidable incident of modern life (Carpenter). Failing all three, it is an ordinary business record of bilateral dealings, governed by Miller/Smith and the reasonableness limits on compulsory process (Oklahoma Press). Prospective location streams are governed in real time by Knotts/Karo and cross into category (3) at the convergence point. We concede, because every honest lawyer must, that category (3) has contestable margins — six days, five — but contestable margins attend every standard this Court administers, including Petitioner's chronicle/ledger rule, whose margin is the whole field (Part One, Q26). A framework with eight years of consistent lower-court administration behind it is not a reservation wrapped in a promise; it is doctrine in ordinary operation.

### Q30. This record shows the statutory safeguard defeated by one checkbox and the statutory deterrent apparently never used. If we reaffirm Miller and affirm, what do we tell the citizen who asks what, in 2026, the Fourth Amendment still protects that a subpoena and a fax form cannot reach?

**Answer:** Tell the citizen the truth, which is more protective than the question implies. The Fourth Amendment in 2026 places beyond any subpoena or fax form: the interior of his home and its curtilage (Kyllo, Karo, Florida v. Jardines, 569 U.S. 1 (2013)); his person and the contents of his phone (Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)); the contents of his letters, emails, and conversations wherever stored (Jackson, Katz, Warshak); and the whole of his physical movements — the archive of his life — whether compiled by GPS, by historical CSLI, or by a real-time stream run long enough to become that archive (Jones, Carpenter, and the convergence principle the Government has accepted throughout these answers). What the fax form reached in this case was none of those things: two fixes showing a phone in open wetlands and at a public service station — facts any officer parked on State Road 9336 could have observed without anyone's permission. And tell the citizen one thing more: the checkbox that was abused here is a *statutory* gate that Congress can rebuild next session — verification, judicial review, durational limits, real penalties — and this Court keeps that repair possible by declining to freeze the entire field into constitutional law on the facts of one rogue detective. The Fourth Amendment protects what is private. The political branches protect more. This case asks the Court to confuse the two, and the right answer to the citizen — and to the question presented — is to keep them distinct and keep them both.
