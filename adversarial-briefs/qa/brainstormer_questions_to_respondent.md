# Questions from the Bench (Neutral) — To Respondent
## Dexter Morgan v. United States, No. 850-418 — Q&A Phase

*Thirty questions from a neutral, probing bench. Petitioner's interrogatories already demand the October 10 act, the Karo quotation, and the duration number; these questions press one level up — the opinion the Government would have us write and the next case it decides, the voluntariness premise the Government half-abandons, the incentive structure of its exigency theory, a right left without any working remedy, and what the third-party doctrine still means in 2026.*

---

### I. The Opinion You'd Have Us Write: Real-Time Data and the Next Case

**Q1.** Dictate the holding that affirms you — the sentence lower courts will apply — and tell us whether "short-term, targeted" is measured in hours, in fixes, or in suspects, because the next case will be six days, not three.

**Q2.** If the constitutional harm is the retrospective archive, then a real-time stream becomes a search the moment its accumulated fixes would, if requested as historical records, trigger *Carpenter*. Do you accept that convergence point — and if not, why does the same archive change constitutional character depending on the direction from which it was assembled?

**Q3.** What result for a tower dump — *Carpenter*'s other reservation — under the rationale you press today: thousands of bystanders, one moment, public places, a search as to none of them?

**Q4.** An FBI-operated cell-site simulator captures the same fixes with no carrier in the chain; is that a search? If your answer differs from this case, the third-party doctrine is doing all the work you elsewhere assign to *Knotts* — which is it?

**Q5.** Carriers can now generate AI-assembled "pattern of life" summaries from the same tower data on demand; under your rule, is a request for the summary a search while a request for the underlying real-time fixes is not — and what sense does that distinction make?

**Q6.** Would the Government make this same no-search argument for a thirty-day real-time stream confined entirely to public places? If no, name the day the Constitution attaches; if yes, tell us what remains of *Carpenter*'s "whole of physical movements."

**Q7.** Is footnote 3's reservation a one-way ratchet — available to defeat Petitioner's a fortiori argument but never to support protection — or do you concede the reservation leaves us free to rule against you on real-time data without disturbing a single precedent?

### II. Voluntariness, Knowing Exposure, and the Government's Own Hedge

**Q8.** You take the SCA's reliance benefit while resisting its expectation cost: § 2702 forbids the carrier to disclose, yet you say the customer "knowingly exposes" the data. Can the Government hold both positions, and which one do you surrender?

**Q9.** Does the Government accept *Warshak* — that email content held by a provider requires a warrant? If yes, state the principle by which content conveyed to a provider's servers is protected while location generated to a provider's towers is not.

**Q10.** Your lead theory is *Knotts*, with voluntariness expressly "secondary." If *Knotts* decides the case, why are you asking us to reaffirm *Miller* rather than reserving it — and if you need *Miller* reaffirmed, then defend voluntariness for a fix the customer never makes, never sees, and cannot refuse.

**Q11.** *Smith* presumed callers know operators see dialed numbers; what must the 2026 phone user be presumed to know — and does your doctrine require us to attribute to every American the network knowledge of a telecommunications engineer?

**Q12.** If voluntariness is a fiction the Government no longer defends, the doctrine you ask us to reaffirm stands on one leg — knowing exposure — and *Carpenter* cut that leg for location data. What exactly are we reaffirming, other than the word "*Miller*"?

### III. Exigency Architecture: The Stream, the Statute, and the Incentive

**Q13.** Draft the exigency paragraph of our opinion so that it does not teach officers to open streams first and await emergencies. Can it be done — and if your answer depends on after-the-fact suppression litigation, isn't the warrant the cheaper teacher?

**Q14.** Your episode-by-episode theory makes each arriving fix a separate acquisition; were the October 8 and 9 fixes therefore dozens of discrete Fourth Amendment violations — and why does the Government get to count its lawful episodes but never total its unlawful ones?

**Q15.** If exigency attached on October 10, did it expire at the drive-thru arrest? Was the stream still running after Morgan was in custody, under a certification no one ever rescinded — and whose problem is that under your theory?

**Q16.** How many § 2702(b)(8) emergency requests do carriers receive each year, and what fraction are ever audited or found false? If the Government cannot tell us, why should we calibrate constitutional doctrine to the assumption that Doakes is rare?

**Q17.** Should Verizon have done anything differently here — or does the Government's position imply the carrier behaved exactly as the system intends when it tracked a man for days on one unverified checkbox?

**Q18.** The danger-of-death standard appears both in the statute you stipulated away and in the constitutional argument you now press; if October 10's facts satisfy the Fourth Amendment's emergency threshold, why did the Government stipulate they fail the statute's identical one — what daylight between the two standards are you standing in?

### IV. Rights Without Remedies

**Q19.** Walk me to the end of your remedial road: no suppression under Part IV, § 2707 damages against a likely judgment-proof officer behind qualified immunity, and no *Bivens* claim after *Egbert* against the federal agents. Who, concretely, ever enforces the right you concede may exist?

**Q20.** Name one false-certification case — one — in which an officer was prosecuted, disciplined, or held liable under § 2707. If the answer is silence, your "other means to punish" argument is a hypothesis; why should constitutional doctrine rest on it?

**Q21.** If we hold this was a search but deny suppression on your cost-benefit theory, we announce that warrantless real-time tracking violates the Constitution and costs the government nothing. What does that opinion teach the next Doakes?

**Q22.** Your deterrence argument treats the FBI agents as the relevant actors and Doakes as an outlier — but the certificate, the stream, and the relay were one officer's project. Under *Herring*'s own culpability framework, isn't deliberate falsification the core case for exclusion rather than the exception to it?

**Q23.** Is the Government's true position that the exclusionary rule should never apply to carrier-disclosure violations, constitutional or otherwise? If so, say it plainly, so we can decide whether Congress's silence on suppression was a choice about statutory violations or a choice about the Fourth Amendment.

### V. Stare Decisis on the Government's Own Terms

**Q24.** Yes or no: is *Miller*'s reasoning — disclosure to anyone, for any purpose, forfeits protection against everyone — correct on its own terms in 2026? If you cannot say yes, you are asking us to retain a precedent on reliance alone; when have we done that, and should we?

**Q25.** Your reliance case is legislative — the SCA — but statutes are written against moving constitutional floors all the time, and *Carpenter* moved this one without collapse. Name the concrete enforcement program that fails if we hold only that government-commanded prospective tracking requires a warrant.

**Q26.** If Congress is the right calibrator, state the constitutional minimum below which Congress may not legislate — or is the answer zero, so that the Fourth Amendment's application to the digital records of three hundred million people is wholly a matter of legislative grace?

**Q27.** Petitioner's wrecking ball never swings if you concede the narrow point that commanded prospective tracking is a search; the Government refuses even that. Why — what does the United States actually lose in the next ten years from a warrant rule confined to government-initiated real-time location?

### VI. The Third-Party Doctrine's 2026 Footprint

**Q28.** Apply the doctrine you defend to this Term's technologies: Alexa audio, smart-meter occupancy inference, a wearable's biometric stream, an AI assistant's conversation logs, a consumer DNA database. Which, if any, does the Government concede are protected — and under what test, announced where?

**Q29.** Prosecutors and magistrates need to know when *Miller* governs and when *Carpenter* does; state the Government's operative test — depth, breadth, comprehensiveness, voluntariness, something else — or concede that the "stable framework" you defend is a reservation wrapped in a narrowness promise.

**Q30.** Last question: this record shows the statutory safeguard defeated by one checkbox and the statutory deterrent apparently never used. If we nonetheless reaffirm *Miller* and affirm this judgment, what do we tell the citizen who asks what, in 2026, the Fourth Amendment still protects that a subpoena and a fax form cannot reach?
