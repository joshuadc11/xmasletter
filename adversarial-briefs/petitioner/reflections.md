# Petitioner War-Room Log — Morgan v. United States (Issue I)

## Round 1

### (a) Theory of the case and strategic choices

**Core theory:** "A stalker with a badge turned a citizen's own phone into a tracking beacon, and the only thing standing between him and a magistrate was a fax form he filled out falsely." The case is the third-party doctrine's own reductio ad absurdum: the doctrine's defenders say statutory safeguards (the SCA) make the warrant requirement unnecessary, and this record shows those safeguards failing completely — both parties *stipulate* the SCA emergency exception does not apply.

**Three nested rings of relief (deliberate fallback architecture):**
1. **Narrowest:** *Carpenter* a fortiori — real-time, multi-day, GPS-grade tracking is a search even if Miller stands. The Court can rule for us without touching stare decisis.
2. **Middle:** Third-party doctrine inapplicable *on its own terms* — no voluntary conveyance because the data didn't exist until Verizon generated it at police command (*Almonor* agency theory). This is, I think, our strongest analytical move: it doesn't ask the Court to overrule anything, it asks the Court to read *Miller*'s own predicates honestly.
3. **Broadest (the actual QP):** Overrule *Miller* via the conventional stare decisis factors (*Janus*/*Ramos* framework): poor reasoning (secrecy ≠ privacy; counterfeit assumption of risk), unworkability (Carpenter's carve-out jurisprudence is a slow-motion overruling), doctrinal erosion, changed facts, no legitimate reliance.

**Key factual framings I committed to:**
- "Multi-day surveillance, not a single ping" — Doakes wrote "current" with no end date and received data "for the next several days." This neutralizes the Government's best move (analogizing to a one-time exigent ping cases).
- Doakes's bad faith front and center: extrajudicial stalking "for weeks," pending professional complaint by Morgan, false emergency certification. Legally, *Katz* is an objective test and officer motive shouldn't matter — but atmospherically it makes the "police need flexibility" argument radioactive, and it directly rebuts any good-faith/reasonableness undertone.
- I leaned on the record's own phrase "the GPS data provided by Verizon" (from the suppression-motion paragraph) to claim GPS-level precision rather than coarse CSLI. This is textually supported but the Government will contest it (see (c)).
- The stipulation that the SCA exception does NOT apply does double duty: it removes the statutory escape hatch AND proves the unreliability of statutory substitutes for the warrant requirement (Argument III.F).

**What I deliberately did NOT argue:** exclusionary rule / good-faith-exception remedies (not in the QP; if the Government pivots to *Davis v. United States* good-faith reliance, I'll address it in Round 2 — the stipulation and Doakes's false certification are my answers); standing/trespass theory under *Jones* property framework (no physical trespass here, and stretching it would look weak); Issue 2 (forbidden).

### (b) Weaknesses I'm most worried about

1. **The fruits are thin even if we win the search question.** The pings only told police roughly where Morgan was; the murder conviction rests on Scuteri's maps built from "the cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier" — if any of that was historical CSLI under seven days, *Carpenter* footnote 3 arguably leaves it unprotected, and the Government may argue independent or attenuated sources (car search incident to arrest, the Everglades crime scene found "a few hours later"). I framed relief as reverse-and-remand to avoid overpromising suppression of everything. But strictly, fruits/remedy may be outside the QP — I should be ready at "oral argument" anyway.
2. **The QP is maximalist and the Court hates maximalism.** Asking to overrule *Miller* outright invites "we can resolve this narrowly" — which is fine for Morgan but a loss on the actual question presented. My nested structure tries to make even the narrow win feel like a repudiation of the doctrine, but a disciplined respondent will exploit the gap between "Morgan wins" and "Miller falls."
3. **Precision of the data.** The majority opinion calls it CSLI from pings with a 30-minute delay; "GPS data" appears once, in the procedural-history sentence. If the Government characterizes this as coarse, delayed, near-historical CSLI (a 30-min delay arguably makes each ping a *stored record* by the time police see it!), my "live homing beacon" imagery weakens. The 30-minute-delay-makes-it-historical argument is genuinely clever and I expect them to make it.
4. **Public-roads exposure.** Every data point in the record (Everglades road, service station, drive-thru) is a public place. *Knotts* says no reasonable expectation of privacy in movements on public thoroughfares. My answer — the *technique* is indifferent to public/private and *could* have reached the home (*Karo*/*Kyllo*), plus *Carpenter* rejected the public-movements rationale for phone tracking — is good but not airtight; *Carpenter* itself reserved real-time CSLI.
5. **Reliance interests are stronger than I conceded.** Thousands of statutes, subpoena practices, grand-jury doctrine, and everyday law enforcement (bank records, toll records, utility records) are built on *Miller*/*Smith*. Overruling outright could unsettle all of it. My "no legitimate reliance in unconstitutional practices" move is rhetorically strong but *Dobbs*/*Janus*-style reliance analysis usually weighs concrete systemic reliance heavily. I may need a limiting principle in Round 2: e.g., overrule as to *content-rich digital records*, retain a subpoena-reasonableness framework.
6. **Officer misconduct cuts both ways.** The Government can say: this is a rogue-officer problem solved by statutory penalties (SCA civil remedies, departmental discipline), not a reason to rewrite Fourth Amendment doctrine for the honest officer.

### (c) Predicted Respondent arguments

1. **Knotts/public-movements:** Morgan was tracked only on public roads, for two relevant pings; no expectation of privacy in public movements; *Carpenter* explicitly preserved *Knotts* and reserved real-time CSLI.
2. **Quantity/mosaic minimalism:** This was a handful of coarse pings over ~2 days, nowhere near *Carpenter*'s seven days or *Jones*'s four weeks; the "mosaic" never formed. They'll quote the majority below: less scope and sensitivity than Riley/Jones.
3. **The 30-minute delay = quasi-historical records** voluntarily generated by the phone's ordinary operation and held briefly by Verizon — i.e., business records squarely within *Smith*/*Miller* and within *Carpenter*'s reservation.
4. **Stare decisis fortress:** *Carpenter* itself "declined to extend" but pointedly preserved *Miller* and *Smith*; massive government and legislative reliance (subpoenas, grand juries, AML/KYC banking law, pen registers); workability cuts their way (bright-line rule vs. my case-by-case Katz inquiry); the proper forum for calibrating digital privacy is Congress, which already acted (the SCA).
5. **Voluntary conveyance still works:** Morgan chose to carry a powered-on phone knowing carriers can locate it; Verizon used its own network facilities; *Almonor* is a state-constitutional decision (Mass. Declaration of Rights art. 14) with limited persuasive force on the federal question.
6. **Backstop remedies:** even if a search, exclusion is inappropriate — good-faith reliance on the SCA's facially applicable emergency process (*Illinois v. Krull* / *Davis* line), inevitable discovery (Lundy's FBI manhunt, the park rangers' independent discovery of the crime scene), attenuation. NOTE: the stipulation says the SCA good-faith exception doesn't apply, but the Government may argue that stipulation governs the *statute*, not the *constitutional* exclusionary-rule good-faith doctrine. I need a crisp answer on the difference.
7. Possibly: Morgan, as a forensic analyst, had *reduced* subjective expectation of privacy because he knew exactly how carrier location works. (Weak, but expect it as color.)

### (d) Open questions for the brainstormer

1. **The 30-minute delay problem:** What's the best doctrinal answer to "each ping became a stored/historical record before police saw it, so this is just sub-7-day historical CSLI that Carpenter left alone"? Is the answer that the *acquisition* (compelled prospective generation) is the search, regardless of delivery latency?
2. **Limiting principle for overruling:** If the Court asks "what replaces Miller — does the government now need a warrant for every grand-jury subpoena to a bank?", what is our cleanest rule? Options: (i) pure Katz totality with third-party status as one factor; (ii) a "content-rich digital records" category; (iii) Kerr-style distinction between records *about* the customer's life vs. the business's own transaction records. Which sells best to this Court?
3. **Stipulation scope:** How hard can I lean on "the SCA good-faith/emergency exception does not apply" as also foreclosing the *constitutional* good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule? Is there a principled bridge, or should I preemptively argue exclusion on falsified-certification grounds (no objectively reasonable reliance on a warrantless process the officer himself corrupted)?
4. **Agency theory risk:** Does pressing *Almonor* agency theory too hard concede that *ordinary* (non-commanded) CSLI collection IS protected by the third-party doctrine — undercutting the overruling argument? How do I keep ring 2 and ring 3 from eating each other?
5. **Should I invoke the original-meaning/positive-law angle** (Gorsuch's *Carpenter* dissent: bailment/property in one's data, SCA as positive law creating a protected interest) to attract that wing of the Court, or does flirting with the property framework muddy a clean Katz-based brief?
6. **Fact check for Round 2:** the problem says the form was signed "October 8th, 2010" though the bodies were found in 2007 and the trial record reads 2007-era — likely a typo in the problem. Any way Respondent exploits the chronology, and should I flag or ignore it?

2. **New Part I.B: a real Knotts answer.** Round 1 had only the Karo "indifferent to the threshold" move, which the brainstormer correctly noted has a hole (Karo turned on actual in-home revelation). Now three layers: (i) *Knotts* itself reserved "dragnet-type" 24-hour surveillance (460 U.S. at 283–84); (ii) *Carpenter* already rejected the public-movements rationale for phone tracking ("whole of their physical movements"); (iii) the search is the compelled acquisition, not the post hoc geography of where fixes landed — which also answers their *Oliver*/*Hester* open-fields pairing ("The Everglades did not report Morgan's location. 3. **New Part I.C: the Rule 41(b)(4)/§ 3117 structural argument** answering the 30-minute-delay "quasi-historical records" reframe I predicted in Round 1 and Respondent duly made. Congress classifies prospective location monitoring as tracking-device-warrant territory; latency can't convert surveillance into record production (Berger wiretap analogy).
4. **Surfaced the fallback as its own heading.** Part II is now expressly titled "At minimum..." so the narrow path appears in the table of contents, and is framed a fortiori ("even courts that accept Miller must concede...") per the brainstormer's advice on keeping ring 2 from eating ring 3. 5. **New Part III.B: the Kerr-style limiting principle.** Customer-activity chronicles (protected) vs. the business's own transactional ledgers (Oklahoma Press subpoena reasonableness, unchanged). 6. **Kimble rebuttal added** (III intro): their "special justification / enhanced force" authority is statutory-stare-decisis law; Miller is constitutional, where Payne says stare decisis is weakest. 8. **New Part IV: full exigency rebuttal** replacing Round 1's single sentence. ### (b) Responding to the opposition

- **What worried me most: the exigency backstop.** It's their cheapest path to affirmance — the Court can duck the doctrine entirely and say "serial killer, reasonable search." The brainstormer's three record points (freeze-out, Oct. 8 vs. Oct. 10, Mincey's actual holding) convert this from my weakest flank into possibly my strongest section. "What was the emergency on October 8th?" is now *my* question, not theirs.
- **Second worry: the 30-minute-delay reframe.** Genuinely clever — it slots the case into Carpenter footnote 3's reserved sub-seven-day historical category. The Rule 41(b)(4)/§ 3117 structural answer is the right kill: it's Congress's own taxonomy, so it appeals to exactly the Justices who like their SCA-reliance argument.
- **Third: the quarter-mile.** They caught my "GPS-grade" overreach. Rather than defend it, I retreated to defensible ground: separated the datasets, recharacterized precision as "precise enough to intercept a moving car," and made the trial maps a fruits problem.
- **Their stare decisis fortress** I now meet with the Kimble misapplication (a credibility wound the brainstormer flagged for them too — if they fix it in Round 2, my point still stands on Payne), the limiting principle, and the Carpenter-already-amputated-§ 2703(d) empirics.
- **Anticipated Round 2 moves I pre-positioned against:** ping-by-ping severability (answered in IV: the Oct. 10 fixes were "the continuing yield of the same unauthorized stream"; exigency measured when the search is undertaken); the no-suppression-remedy promotion (Leon/Franks falsified-predicate sentences); Hammond/Riley-6th-Cir. short-term-tracking cases (my "multi-day, no end date, no predicate" framing distinguishes them factually — those were hours-long fugitive locates; I chose not to cite them by name and invite the comparison, but I'm ready orally).
