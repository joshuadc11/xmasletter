## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should overrule *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and invalidate the third-party doctrine, where law enforcement — without a warrant, without probable cause, and on the strength of a falsely certified "emergency" form — conscripted a cell-service provider into tracking a citizen's real-time location for days on end.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

For days in October, a detective with a personal vendetta held in his hands a power that the Framers fought a revolution to deny the Crown: the ability to follow a citizen everywhere he went, without ever leaving his desk and without ever facing a magistrate. Detective James Doakes — who, the record establishes, had been "extrajudicially stalking" Petitioner for weeks before he "lost track of his target" — faxed Verizon a form falsely certifying a danger of "death or serious bodily injury." Both parties stipulate that no emergency existed and that no statutory exception applies. On that false certification alone, Verizon pinged Petitioner's phone and delivered his real-time location to law enforcement, again and again, over multiple days. No warrant. No probable cause. No judge. The decision below blessed this surveillance on a single ground: the third-party doctrine of *Miller* and *Smith v. Maryland*.

This Court should reverse, for three nested reasons.

*First*, *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), already establishes that warrantless cell-phone location tracking of this character is a search. Everything that troubled the Court about historical CSLI — its comprehensiveness, its intimacy, its effortlessness, its inescapability — is present here in aggravated form. Real-time tracking is more intrusive than the historical records in *Carpenter*, not less: it converts the phone into a live homing beacon on the person, reaching wherever he goes, including spaces the Fourth Amendment has always protected absolutely. *United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984).

*Second*, the third-party doctrine cannot apply on its own terms. The doctrine rests on *voluntary conveyance* of information to a third party *in the ordinary course of business*. The location data here was never conveyed by Petitioner at all. It did not exist until Verizon, acting at the government's command, generated it by pinging the phone. As the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court recognized in *Commonwealth v. Almonor*, 120 N.E.3d 1183 (Mass. 2019), a carrier that activates a phone's location function at police direction is not a recipient of shared business records — it is an agent of law enforcement. The government cannot launder a search through a private intermediary.

*Third*, and squarely presented by the question on which certiorari was granted: *Miller* should be overruled. Its reasoning was thin when written and is fictional now; its rule has proven unworkable, as *Carpenter*'s open-ended carve-outs demonstrate; the factual world that produced it — paper deposit slips and rotary phones — has vanished; and no legitimate reliance interest is impaired by requiring police to do what they already do thousands of times a year: get a warrant. Every traditional stare decisis factor favors overruling. *Stare decisis* is "not an inexorable command," *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991), and it is at its weakest where a precedent licenses ongoing constitutional violations that legislatures cannot cure.

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE WARRANTLESS, MULTI-DAY, REAL-TIME TRACKING OF PETITIONER'S PHONE WAS A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. Carpenter's logic governs real-time location tracking a fortiori.

The Fourth Amendment's "basic purpose . . . is to safeguard the privacy and security of individuals against arbitrary invasions by governmental officials." *Camara v. Municipal Court*, 387 U.S. 523, 528 (1967). Under *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), that guarantee protects what a person "seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public." And in *Carpenter*, this Court held that accessing cell-phone location records breaches a reasonable expectation of privacy because such records provide "an intimate window into a person's life," achieving "near perfect surveillance" that is "remarkably easy, cheap, and efficient compared to traditional investigative tools." 585 U.S. at 311–12.

Every feature that made historical CSLI a search makes real-time tracking a greater one:

**1. Precision.** The historical cell-site data in *Carpenter* placed a phone within a wedge spanning up to several square miles. The pings here, by contrast, drew on device-level location capabilities accurate enough to place Petitioner's phone in "a remote part of the Everglades" and then at a specific service station ten miles away — precision the Court in *Carpenter* warned was "rapidly approaching GPS-level." 585 U.S. at 313. GPS-level tracking is precisely what five Justices in *United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012), concluded implicates the Fourth Amendment when prolonged, because location data exposes "familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations." *Id.* at 415 (Sotomayor, J., concurring); *see People v. Weaver*, 12 N.Y.3d 433 (N.Y. 2009) (cataloguing the "indisputably private" destinations such data reveals — the psychiatrist, the clinic, the house of worship, the criminal defense attorney).

**2. Duration.** The Government will call this a "single ping" case. The record refutes that. Doakes faxed the form on October 8 and "[f]or the next several days . . . received location data from Morgan's phone." This was a continuous, multi-day tracking operation — the prospective twin of the days-long historical sweep *Carpenter* condemned and the prolonged monitoring that *Jones* condemned. And duration aside, *Carpenter* never held that a single day of CSLI is fair game; it expressly declined to decide "whether there is a limited period for which the Government may obtain" such data. 585 U.S. at 310 n.3.

**3. Reach into protected spaces.** A live tracking beacon does not respect the threshold of the home. This Court held in *Karo* that monitoring a beeper inside a private residence is a search, 468 U.S. at 714–15, and in *Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27 (2001), that technology revealing details of the home's interior is a search. A real-time phone ping reports the phone — and thus the person, for the phone is "almost a 'feature of human anatomy,'" *Carpenter*, 585 U.S. at 311 (quoting *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 385 (2014)) — wherever it is, including the bedroom. Unlike the visual surveillance of a car on public roads in *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), the ping does not stop at the curtilage.

State courts of last resort confronting this exact question have so held. *Tracey v. State*, 152 So. 3d 504 (Fla. 2014) (warrant required for real-time CSLI tracking); *Commonwealth v. Pacheco*, 263 A.3d 626 (Pa. 2021) (pinging a phone for real-time location is a search); *Almonor*, *supra*. The Thirteenth Circuit's contrary holding makes federal courts an outlier against the consistent judgment of the state courts closest to these facts.

#### B. The decision below inverted Carpenter's reasoning.

The court below reasoned that because the surveillance here gathered "less" than the weeks of data in *Jones* or the digital archive in *Riley*, it falls outside the Fourth Amendment. That gets this Court's cases backwards. *Riley* and *Carpenter* did not establish a floor of intrusion below which police may roam freely; they recognized that digital surveillance differs in kind, not merely degree, from pre-digital analogues. The question is not whether Doakes could have gathered still more, but whether society recognizes as reasonable an expectation that the government will not secretly commandeer one's own phone to report one's movements, hour by hour, for days. It does. A contrary rule means that every one of the hundreds of millions of Americans carrying a phone is subject to instantaneous, warrantless location at the unilateral whim of any officer with a fax machine — including, as here, an officer pursuing a personal grudge.

### II. THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE DOES NOT REACH THIS CASE, BECAUSE NOTHING WAS VOLUNTARILY CONVEYED — THE GOVERNMENT CREATED THE DATA IT SEIZED.

Even on its own terms, *Miller* cannot sustain the judgment. The doctrine holds that one who "voluntarily convey[s]" information to a third party "in the ordinary course of business" assumes the risk of its disclosure. 425 U.S. at 442–43; *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735, 744 (1979). Both predicates fail here.

**A. No conveyance.** Miller wrote checks; Smith dialed numbers. Each performed an affirmative act transmitting discrete information to a business that recorded it for its own purposes. Petitioner did nothing. The location fixes at issue were not records Verizon kept in the ordinary course; they were generated on demand, when Verizon pinged the phone *because the government asked it to*. As *Almonor* recognized, such data "would not otherwise be collected and retained by the service provider." 120 N.E.3d at 1194–95. When a carrier activates a phone's location function solely to answer a police demand, it acts as an instrument of the state, and the state's use of a private agent does not strip the target of constitutional protection — otherwise the government could evade *Karo* by asking a private party to plant the beeper.

**B. No voluntariness.** *Carpenter* has already exposed the "voluntary exposure" rationale as a fiction in the cell-phone context: carrying a phone "is indispensable to participation in modern society," and a phone logs location data "without any affirmative act on the part of the user beyond powering up." 585 U.S. at 315. "In no meaningful sense does the user voluntarily 'assume[] the risk'" of turning over a comprehensive record of his movements. *Id.* If that is true of data the phone generates passively, it is doubly true of data the user never generates at all — data conjured into existence by a government-directed ping the user cannot perceive, refuse, or avoid short of abandoning his phone altogether.

### III. MILLER SHOULD BE OVERRULED. EVERY STARE DECISIS FACTOR SUPPORTS RETIRING THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE.

The Court granted certiorari to decide whether *Miller* should be overruled. It should be. Stare decisis "is at its weakest when we interpret the Constitution," because legislative correction is "practically impossible." *Payne*, 501 U.S. at 828. The Court weighs the quality of the precedent's reasoning, its workability, its consistency with related decisions, changed factual circumstances, and reliance. *See Janus v. AFSCME*, 585 U.S. 878 (2018); *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83 (2020). Each factor points the same way.

**A. Quality of reasoning.** *Miller* rests on two errors. It equates secrecy with privacy, holding that any disclosure to anyone forfeits protection against everyone — a proposition *Katz* itself rejected when it protected words spoken into a phone line necessarily carried by a third party. And it rests on a counterfeit "assumption of risk": no one assumes a risk he has no realistic ability to avoid. As Justice Sotomayor observed, the doctrine is "ill suited to the digital age, in which people reveal a great deal of information about themselves to third parties in the course of carrying out mundane tasks." *Jones*, 565 U.S. at 417 (Sotomayor, J., concurring). Privacy is not "an all-or-nothing proposition" — sharing with one's bank or carrier for a limited purpose is not consent to disclosure to the police. *Smith*, 442 U.S. at 749 (Marshall, J., dissenting).

**B. Unworkability.** *Carpenter* tried to save the doctrine by cabining it, and the result is a jurisprudence of unanswerable line-drawing: seven days of CSLI is a search, but how many is not? Historical data is covered, but real-time data, tower dumps, IP logs, smart-home records, and search histories are all "not before us." 585 U.S. at 316. Lower courts — and, worse, officers in the field — are left to guess which categories of digitally intermediated life retain constitutional protection. A doctrine that requires this Court to issue a new technological exception every few years is not a workable rule; it is a slow-motion overruling. The Court should finish the job candidly and replace the categorical carve-out with *Katz*'s ordinary reasonable-expectation inquiry, under which disclosure to a third party is one factor, not a talisman.

**C. Doctrinal erosion.** *Miller* now sits in open tension with *Katz*, *Kyllo*, *Jones*, *Riley*, and above all *Carpenter*, which "decline[d] to extend" it precisely because its rationales fail in the digital context. 585 U.S. at 314. A precedent already hollowed by its progeny has the weakest claim to preservation. *See Ramos*, 590 U.S. at 121–22 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring) (precedent's inconsistency with related decisions supports overruling).

**D. Changed facts.** The 1976 depositor handed his bank a finite stack of paper. The 2026 citizen routes his correspondence, finances, movements, health data, reading, and worship through third-party servers because there is no other way to live. A rule keyed to "what you share is unprotected" now means "almost nothing is protected." What may have been tolerable in 1976 is, as the dissent below put it, "profoundly out of date in 2026."

**E. Reliance.** The government has no cognizable reliance interest in warrantless access to private papers; the Fourth Amendment exists to defeat exactly that expectation. The warrant requirement is familiar, administrable, and already embedded in the SCA's own architecture, which contemplates warrants as the default and preserves a true exigency exception. Nothing about overruling *Miller* disarms police in genuine emergencies — it simply requires that the emergency be real. Here, by stipulation, it was not.

**F. This case shows why the doctrine is dangerous.** The third-party doctrine's defenders promise that statutory safeguards will police the gap. This case is the refutation. The SCA's emergency pathway required a genuine threat of death or serious injury. Doakes — weeks into an obsessive, unauthorized campaign of personal surveillance of a co-worker who had filed a stalking complaint against him — simply checked "YES" on a fax form. Verizon complied within days. No neutral magistrate ever stood between an angry man with a badge and the continuous location of his target, because under the decision below, the Constitution did not require one. "The Founders did not fight a revolution to gain the right to government agency protocols." *Riley*, 573 U.S. at 398. They fought it to put a judge between the executive and the people's private lives. At an absolute minimum, the Court should hold the doctrine inapplicable to government-initiated, real-time location tracking; on the question it granted, it should overrule *Miller* and restore *Katz* as the governing standard.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Thirteenth Circuit should be reversed, the third-party doctrine of *Miller* overruled or held inapplicable, and the case remanded with instructions that the warrantless real-time location surveillance of Petitioner violated the Fourth Amendment.
