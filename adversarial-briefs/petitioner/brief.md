## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should overrule *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and invalidate the third-party doctrine, where a detective — acting without a warrant, without probable cause, and on the strength of a falsely certified "emergency" — conscripted a cell-service provider into reporting a citizen's real-time location, ping after ping, for days.

## STATEMENT OF THE CASE

When the FBI concluded that the Bay Harbor Butcher was likely a Miami Metro insider, it "froze all Miami Metro detectives and personnel out of the investigation." Detective James Doakes was therefore not hunting the Butcher in October; he was forbidden to. What he was doing, the record states, was "extrajudicially stalking Morgan for weeks" — conduct that had already produced a physical altercation in the office and a professional complaint by Morgan, who reported fearing for his and his sister's safety.

On October 8, having "finally lost track of his target," Doakes "in frustration" faxed Verizon an "Emergency Situation Disclosure" form, certifying a "danger of death or serious bodily injury" and requesting "Location Information" for the time frame "current," with no end date. Both parties stipulate that the Stored Communications Act's emergency provision, 18 U.S.C. § 2702, does not apply. "For the next several days," Verizon delivered Morgan's location to Doakes. No active crime scene existed anywhere in the case until October 10 — two days after the tracking began — when park rangers, not Doakes, discovered the Everglades site.

Two datasets matter and must not be conflated. *First*, the real-time fixes streamed to Doakes from October 8 onward — precise enough to trace a moving car along State Road 9336 and direct federal agents to a particular drive-thru. *Second*, the "cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier" that Agent Scuteri mapped at trial, placing the phone "within a quarter mile" of the murder scene. The quarter-mile figure describes the trial exhibits, not the tracking. And the trial exhibits exist only because the tracking located, stopped, and charged Morgan in the first place. *Cf. Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

*First*, under *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), the multi-day, open-ended, government-initiated real-time tracking of Petitioner's phone was a search. Everything that troubled the Court about historical CSLI is present here in aggravated form, and neither *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), the hours-long fugitive locates in the Government's circuit cases, nor the thirty-minute delivery delay changes that: *Knotts* reserved precisely the open-ended monitoring at issue, *Carpenter* refused to extend the public-movements rationale to phone tracking, and Congress itself classifies prospective location monitoring as warrant territory, Fed. R. Crim. P. 41(b)(4); 18 U.S.C. § 3117.

*Second*, even if *Miller* survives, its doctrine cannot reach this case. The third-party doctrine presupposes voluntary conveyance of records a business keeps for its own purposes. These fixes were never conveyed by anyone; they were generated on government command. At minimum, the Court should so hold.

*Third*, on the question granted, *Miller* should be overruled — with a limiting principle. The Fourth Amendment should protect records that chronicle the customer's own protected activities; it need not disturb compelled production of a business's own transactional ledgers, which remain governed by ordinary subpoena reasonableness. *Oklahoma Press Publishing Co. v. Walling*, 327 U.S. 186 (1946). That rule answers every workability and reliance objection the Government raises.

*Fourth*, no exigency saves this search. The only warrantless act was undertaken on October 8 — two days before any crime scene was known to exist — and the government took no new action on October 10 that an exigency could excuse. *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), forecloses the gravity-of-the-offense theory, and the SCA's remedial scheme supplies no exit: 18 U.S.C. § 2708 is exclusive, by its own text, only as to "nonconstitutional violations."

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE WARRANTLESS, MULTI-DAY, REAL-TIME TRACKING OF PETITIONER'S PHONE WAS A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. Carpenter's logic governs real-time tracking a fortiori.

*Carpenter* held that government access to cell-phone location records invades a reasonable expectation of privacy because such records provide "an intimate window into a person's life" and make surveillance "easy, cheap, and efficient compared to traditional investigative tools." 585 U.S. at 311. Every feature that made historical CSLI a search makes real-time tracking a greater one. The phone is "almost a 'feature of human anatomy,'" *id.* (quoting *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 385 (2014)); a live tracking stream converts it into a homing beacon attached to the person. Five Justices in *United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012), concluded that longer-term GPS monitoring of a person's movements impinges on expectations of privacy, *id.* at 415 (Sotomayor, J., concurring); *id.* at 430 (Alito, J., concurring in the judgment), because location data exposes one's "familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations." *Id.* at 415.

The Government now concedes the duration: Doakes requested data for the time frame "current," with no end date, and "[f]or the next several days . . . received location data from Morgan's phone" — a continuous, open-ended tracking operation, the prospective twin of the historical sweep condemned in *Carpenter*. The Government answers that *Carpenter*'s engine was the *retrospective* archive — the power to "travel back in time" — which a forward-looking stream lacks. But retrospectivity was an aggravator, not the holding. The holding is a reasonable expectation of privacy "in the whole of [one's] physical movements," 585 U.S. at 313, and a live stream invades that whole more completely, not less: it adds what no archive contains — the suspect's location *now* — and becomes an archive as it runs; by October 10, Doakes held days of accumulated movements, the very aggregate *Carpenter* protects. Nor is the Court's reservation of real-time CSLI, *id.* at 316, a holding in waiting. The Government reasons that the reservation "would have been pointless" if *Carpenter*'s logic reached real-time tracking; on that theory, every reservation would be a ruling in disguise. The Court declined to "express a view on matters not before us" — and declined equally to bless *any* period of unprotected access, *id.* at 310 n.3. The reservation leaves the question open; *Carpenter*'s reasoning answers it.

Cell phones are "indispensable to participation in modern society." *Id.* at 315. Under the decision below, every American carrying one is subject to instantaneous, open-ended location by any officer with a fax machine — including, as here, an officer pursuing a personal grudge against the colleague who had filed a stalking complaint against him. State courts of last resort confronting this technology have required a warrant. *Tracey v. State*, 152 So. 3d 504 (Fla. 2014); *Commonwealth v. Pacheco*, 263 A.3d 626 (Pa. 2021); *Commonwealth v. Almonor*, 120 N.E.3d 1183 (Mass. 2019). The Government discounts them as state-constitutional; their shared account of what reasonable people expect remains the touchstone of *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967).

#### B. Knotts does not control, Carpenter rejected its rationale for phones, and the Government's circuit cases involve a different practice.

The Government rests its no-search case on *Knotts*, the open-fields cases, and two courts of appeals. Four answers.

*Knotts answered Knotts.* The Court there cautioned that "dragnet-type law enforcement practices" — "twenty-four hour surveillance of any citizen" — would present a different constitutional question. 460 U.S. at 283–84. An authorization with no end date *is* twenty-four-hour surveillance; that it trains on one citizen rather than many is no comfort to the one. The reserved case is this case, not the beeper-assisted tail of a single drive.

*Carpenter answered Knotts too.* The Government in *Carpenter* made precisely this argument — location data merely captures movements exposed to public view — and the Court rejected it, holding that individuals maintain a reasonable expectation of privacy "in the whole of their physical movements," and distinguishing *Knotts* as "limited" short-term monitoring. 585 U.S. at 310, 313–15. The public-movements rationale does not survive contact with a technology that tracks everyone, everywhere, effortlessly.

*Hammond and Riley answer neither.* Both were hours-long operations to locate identified, dangerous suspects. In *United States v. Hammond*, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021), officers tracked an armed-robbery suspect for a matter of hours, and the Seventh Circuit rested in the alternative on a genuine, contemporaneous exigency — the very thing absent on October 8. *United States v. Riley*, 858 F.3d 1012 (6th Cir. 2017), located a wanted fugitive in roughly seven hours; it predates *Carpenter* and rests on the circuit's earlier reasoning in *United States v. Skinner*, 690 F.3d 772 (6th Cir. 2012), whose public-movements premise *Carpenter* then refused to extend to cell phones. Neither blesses an open-ended, multi-day stream opened by falsified certificate, on no predicate beyond an officer's frustration at losing sight of a colleague. If anything, those cases mark the line this one crosses.

*The search is the acquisition, not the geography.* The legality of compelled surveillance cannot turn on the after-the-fact luck of where the fixes happened to land. When Doakes faxed the form, he could not know — and did not care — whether the next ping would issue from a highway, a church, or a bedroom. A technique whose very design is indifferent to the home's threshold is governed by *United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705, 714–16 (1984), and *Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27 (2001), not by a post hoc audit of coordinates. *Oliver* and *Hester* concern what officers may observe in open terrain; no officer observed anything here. The Everglades did not report Morgan's location. His own phone did, on government command.

#### C. The thirty-minute delay does not convert surveillance into business records. Congress itself says so.

The Government suggests each ping became a quasi-historical "stored record" during its thirty-minute transit. Congress's own architecture refutes the move. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(b)(4) provides warrants for tracking devices — defined functionally as anything "which permits the tracking of the movement of a person or object," 18 U.S.C. § 3117(b) — because prospective location monitoring is surveillance. The Government answers that the Rule reaches only government-installed devices while carrier-derived data belongs to the SCA. But the SCA's compelled-disclosure provisions govern records that exist; nothing in them charters an order that a carrier *generate* a forward-looking location stream. On the Government's reading, an officer unwilling to seek the warrant Rule 41 requires need only commandeer the carrier to do the tracking for him — form-over-substance evasion this Court consistently rejects. And latency cannot transform surveillance into record production: a wiretapped conversation reviewed the next morning is no less an interception. The Fourth Amendment event is the compelled prospective acquisition, not the delivery time.

### II. AT MINIMUM, THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE CANNOT REACH GOVERNMENT-INITIATED, REAL-TIME LOCATION TRACKING, BECAUSE NOTHING WAS VOLUNTARILY CONVEYED.

Even courts and litigants who accept *Miller* must concede it cannot reach commanded generation. The doctrine applies to information a person "voluntarily conveyed" to a business that records it for its own purposes. *Miller*, 425 U.S. at 442; *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735, 744 (1979). Miller wrote checks; Smith dialed numbers; the government later sought the business's existing records. Here the fixes did not exist until Verizon located the phone *because the government asked it to* — data that "would not otherwise be collected and retained by the service provider." *Almonor*, 120 N.E.3d 1183. The Government answers that carriers always know which tower serves a handset. True, and beside the point: passive tower registration is not the on-demand pinging this record describes. A carrier that activates a device's location function solely to answer a police demand is the government's instrument. Were it otherwise, the State could evade *Karo* by asking a private party to plant and monitor the beeper.

The Government's rejoinder — the *Knotts* beeper was government-installed, involved no conveyance at all, and still was no search — defends the wrong doctrine. *Knotts* asked what the surveillance *revealed*; on that question *Carpenter* now governs phone tracking, and Part I answers it. The third-party doctrine's office is different: an *independent* justification resting on the customer's having volunteered the information. If the carrier's identity is beside the point and the government simply stands in the surveiller's shoes, the doctrine does no work here, and the case rises or falls on Part I — where the Government loses.

Nor was anything voluntary. *Carpenter* held that location data is not "shared" in any meaningful sense, because a phone logs its position "without any affirmative act on the part of the user beyond powering up," and "in no meaningful sense does the user voluntarily 'assume the risk.'" 585 U.S. at 315. That reasoning applies doubly to fixes the user never generates at all and cannot perceive, refuse, or avoid. Congress confirms the expectation's reasonableness: 18 U.S.C. § 2702(a) affirmatively *forbids* carriers to divulge these records absent specified process — a positive-law interest in nondisclosure. *Cf. Carpenter*, 585 U.S. 296 (Gorsuch, J., dissenting). The Court can resolve this case on this ground alone, without disturbing a single precedent.

### III. ON THE QUESTION GRANTED, MILLER SHOULD BE OVERRULED — AND A LIMITED SUCCESSOR RULE IS READY AT HAND.

*Stare decisis* is "not an inexorable command," and it is "at its weakest when we interpret the Constitution," because correction by Congress is practically impossible. *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991). The Government's lead authority, *Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC*, 576 U.S. 446 (2015), is miscast: *Kimble*'s "enhanced force" attaches to *statutory* precedents precisely because the political branches can fix them. *Miller* is a constitutional holding. The Government cannot simultaneously insist that Congress is the proper custodian of digital privacy and invoke a constitutional precedent that strips Congress's handiwork of any constitutional floor.

**A. Miller's reasoning has not aged; it has collapsed.** The decision equates secrecy with privacy — disclosure to anyone, for any purpose, forfeits protection against everyone — a proposition *Katz* itself rejected when it protected words spoken into a phone line necessarily carried by a third party. And its "assumption of risk" is counterfeit: no one assumes a risk he has no realistic ability to avoid. *Smith*, 442 U.S. at 749–50 (Marshall, J., dissenting); *Jones*, 565 U.S. at 417 (Sotomayor, J., concurring). *Carpenter* "decline[d] to extend" the doctrine because its twin rationales fail in the digital context, 585 U.S. at 314 — a precedent already hollowed by its own progeny.

**B. The replacement rule: Miller limited to its facts.** The Justices will ask what replaces the doctrine. The answer does not constitutionalize the grand jury. The Fourth Amendment should protect third-party records that are in substance *a chronicle of the customer's own protected activities* — his movements, communications, reading, searching — generated as an unavoidable incident of participation in modern life. It need not disturb compelled production of a business's *own transactional ledgers* documenting voluntary commercial dealings: the bank's record that a check cleared, the toll authority's receipt. Those remain governed by the subpoena-reasonableness framework of *Oklahoma Press*, 327 U.S. 186, which long predates *Miller* and needs nothing from it. The sorting criterion is evidentiary: protection attaches when a record's significance lies in *reconstructing the customer's conduct* rather than in documenting the business's performance of its side of the bargain. Hard cases will come — a credit-card record of a discrete purchase is the merchant bank's ledger entry, and falls on the ledger side — and can be resolved as they arrive, deciding no more than each case requires, as *Carpenter* did. On this rule, *Miller*'s outcome could survive; its talismanic rationale — sharing forfeits everything — falls. That is no retreat from the question granted, which asks whether to invalidate the *doctrine*: the doctrine is the talisman, not the result. This rule answers the workability objection (a categorical line, not case-by-case sensitivity audits) and the parade of horribles: the grand jury investigating securities fraud obtains brokerage records tomorrow as it did the day before *Miller* — under *Oklahoma Press*.

**C. The reliance fortress is already breached — by Carpenter, without incident.** The Government says the SCA was built atop *Miller* and would collapse. But *Carpenter* already invalidated warrantless acquisition of historical CSLI under § 2703(d), and the subpoena system did not collapse; Congress's scheme simply operates above a constitutional floor. Statutes may always grant more protection than the Constitution requires; they cannot define it down. And the government has no cognizable reliance interest in warrantless access to the people's private chronicles — the Fourth Amendment exists to defeat that expectation. Requiring warrants asks police to do what they already do thousands of times a year.

**D. This case is the refutation of the doctrine's promise.** Defenders of *Miller* assure the Court that statutes will police the gap. This record answers them. The SCA's emergency pathway demanded a genuine threat to life. Doakes — frozen out of the investigation, weeks into an obsessive personal-surveillance campaign, the subject of a pending complaint by his target — checked "YES" on a fax form, and a continent-spanning carrier began reporting a citizen's location. No magistrate ever stood between an angry man with a badge and the continuous whereabouts of his quarry. "[T]he Founders did not fight a revolution to gain the right to government agency protocols." *Riley*, 573 U.S. at 398.

### IV. NO EXIGENCY EXCUSED THE WARRANT REQUIREMENT, AND SUPPRESSION FOLLOWS.

The Government now concedes the Court "may assume" the October 8–9 stream was unlawful, retreating to two positions: October 10 was independently reasonable, and suppression is unavailable regardless. Both fail.

**The timeline.** Doakes signed the certification on October 8. The Everglades crime scene was not discovered until October 10. When this tracking began, there was no in-progress murder, no fleeing suspect, no missing person — only a detective who, "in frustration," had "lost track" of the co-worker he was stalking. Exigency is measured by the objective circumstances confronting the officer when the warrantless search is undertaken, *Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141, 149–51 (2013); on October 8 those circumstances were nothing.

**Severability.** Nor can October 10 be carved out as a fresh, exigency-justified acquisition, because exigency excuses a warrantless *act* — and on October 10 the government took none. No officer made any request that day. The fixes simply kept arriving under the October 8 authorization, the one faxed when no emergency existed. Under *McNeely*, the lawfulness of a warrantless acquisition is fixed at its undertaking, and the only undertaking here was October 8. There is no doctrine of retroactive exigency — and for good reason: if an unlawfully opened stream could be redeemed by whatever later flowed through it, every officer would have cause to open the stream first and await the emergency.

**The record.** The FBI had "froze[n] all Miami Metro detectives and personnel out of the investigation"; the manhunt the Government invokes is one its certifying officer was forbidden to conduct. The inquiry is objective, *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 404 (2006) — and objectively, the facts known on October 8 suggested imminent danger to no one. Both parties stipulate no statutory emergency existed; that stipulation does not decide the constitutional question, *cf. Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008) — it corroborates what the timeline independently proves.

**The law.** The Government's own citation condemns its theory. *Mincey v. Arizona* squarely *rejected* a murder-scene exception to the warrant requirement and held that the seriousness of the offense under investigation does not itself create exigency. 437 U.S. at 390–94. That *Mincey* involved a home changes nothing: gravity-is-not-exigency was the holding, and gravity is all the Government had on October 8. Warrants, meanwhile, are obtainable telephonically in minutes. *McNeely*, 569 U.S. at 154–55; *Riley*, 573 U.S. at 401. Nor does a warrant rule endanger the abducted child or the fleeing gunman: the genuine-exigency exception survives untouched, and it — not a diluted definition of "search" — is where the Fourth Amendment houses flexibility. A warrant requirement disarms only the officer whose emergency is invented.

**The remedy.** The Government's no-suppression argument fails at its threshold. Its lead authority, 18 U.S.C. § 2708, makes the SCA's remedies exclusive only for "nonconstitutional violations" of the chapter; by its own text it says nothing about a Fourth Amendment search, and the circuit cases the Government collects hold only that *statutory* violations do not trigger exclusion. Nor is there good faith to weigh in the deterrence balance of *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009), and *Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011): *United States v. Leon* withholds the exception from an officer who falsifies the predicate for the very process he invokes. 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984); *cf. Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154 (1978). And if the Government answers that an officer facing the real October 10 emergency could have obtained the same fixes lawfully — so suppression deters nothing — that is an argument about what the government *could have done*: the vocabulary of independent source and inevitable discovery, with factual predicates to satisfy on remand, not a reason to excuse what it did. The conduct to deter is exactly what happened here — opening an open-ended location stream on a false certificate in the hope that events would later supply the justification. Attenuation and independent-source questions as to particular exhibits are for remand.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Thirteenth Circuit should be reversed. The Court should hold that the warrantless real-time location surveillance of Petitioner was a Fourth Amendment search unsupported by any exigency; overrule *Miller* as to records chronicling a customer's protected activities — or, at minimum, hold the third-party doctrine inapplicable to government-initiated real-time tracking — and remand for further proceedings.
