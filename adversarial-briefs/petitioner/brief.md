## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should overrule *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and invalidate the third-party doctrine, where a detective — acting without a warrant, without probable cause, and on the strength of a falsely certified "emergency" — conscripted a cell-service provider into reporting a citizen's real-time location, ping after ping, for days.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

For several days in October, a detective pursuing a personal vendetta wielded a power the Framers fought a revolution to deny the Crown: the ability to follow a citizen everywhere he went without ever facing a magistrate. Detective James Doakes — who, the record establishes, "had been extrajudicially stalking Morgan for weeks" before he "lost track of his target" — faxed Verizon a form certifying a danger of "death or serious bodily injury." Both parties stipulate that no statutory emergency exception applies. On that false certification alone, Verizon located Petitioner's phone and delivered his real-time position to law enforcement, repeatedly, over multiple days. No warrant. No probable cause. No judge. The court below blessed that surveillance on a single ground: the third-party doctrine of *Miller* and *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979).

This Court should reverse, for three nested reasons.

*First*, *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), already establishes that warrantless cell-phone location tracking of this character is a search. Everything that troubled the Court about historical CSLI — its precision, its intimacy, its effortlessness, its inescapability — is present here in aggravated form. Real-time tracking is more intrusive than historical records, not less: it converts the phone into a live homing beacon attached to the person, reaching wherever he goes, including spaces the Fourth Amendment has always protected absolutely. *United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984).

*Second*, the third-party doctrine cannot apply on its own terms. The doctrine rests on the *voluntary conveyance* of information to a third party in the ordinary course of business. The location fixes here were never conveyed by Petitioner at all. They did not exist until Verizon, acting at the government's command, generated them by locating the phone. As the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court recognized in *Commonwealth v. Almonor*, 120 N.E.3d 1183 (Mass. 2019), a carrier that activates a phone's location function at police direction is not a recipient of shared business records — it is an agent of law enforcement. The government cannot launder a search through a private intermediary.

*Third*, and squarely presented by the question on which certiorari was granted: *Miller* should be overruled. Its reasoning was thin when written and is fictional now; its rule has proven unworkable, as *Carpenter*'s open-ended carve-outs demonstrate; the factual world that produced it — paper deposit slips and rotary phones — has vanished; and no legitimate reliance interest is impaired by requiring police to do what they already do thousands of times a year: get a warrant. *Stare decisis* is "not an inexorable command," *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991), and it is weakest where a precedent licenses ongoing constitutional violations that no legislature can cure.

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE WARRANTLESS, MULTI-DAY, REAL-TIME TRACKING OF PETITIONER'S PHONE WAS A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. Carpenter's logic governs real-time location tracking a fortiori.

The Fourth Amendment's "basic purpose . . . is to safeguard the privacy and security of individuals against arbitrary invasions by governmental officials." *Camara v. Municipal Court*, 387 U.S. 523, 528 (1967). Under *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), that guarantee protects what a person "seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public." *Id.* at 351. And in *Carpenter*, this Court held that government access to cell-phone location records invades a reasonable expectation of privacy because such records provide "an intimate window into a person's life" and achieve a surveillance that is remarkably "easy, cheap, and efficient compared to traditional investigative tools." 585 U.S. at 311.

Every feature that made historical CSLI a search makes real-time tracking a greater one.

**1. Precision.** The historical cell-site data in *Carpenter* placed a phone only within a wedge spanning up to several square miles, and the Court found a search anyway — warning that carrier location technology was "rapidly approaching GPS-level precision." *Id.* The record here describes what Doakes obtained as "the GPS data provided by Verizon": fixes precise enough to place Petitioner's phone first in "a remote part of the Everglades" and then at a particular service station ten miles away, precise enough that agents intercepted him at a specific drive-thru. GPS-grade tracking is exactly what five Justices in *United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012), concluded implicates the Fourth Amendment, because location data exposes one's "familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations." *Id.* at 415 (Sotomayor, J., concurring); *see People v. Weaver*, 12 N.Y.3d 433 (N.Y. 2009) (cataloguing the "indisputably private" destinations such data reveals — the psychiatrist, the clinic, the criminal defense attorney, the house of worship).

**2. Duration.** The Government will call this a single-ping case. The record refutes that. Doakes faxed the form on October 8, requested data with no end date ("current"), and "[f]or the next several days . . . received location data from Morgan's phone." This was a continuous, multi-day, dragnet-style tracking operation — the prospective twin of the multi-day historical sweep condemned in *Carpenter* and the prolonged GPS monitoring condemned in *Jones*. And duration aside, *Carpenter* never held that anything short of seven days is fair game; it expressly declined to decide "whether there is a limited period for which the Government may obtain" location data at all. 585 U.S. 296, 310 n.3.

**3. Reach into protected spaces.** A live tracking beacon does not respect the threshold of the home. This Court held in *Karo* that monitoring a beeper inside a private residence is a search, 468 U.S. at 714–15, and in *Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27 (2001), that sense-enhancing technology revealing details of the home's interior is a search. A real-time ping reports the phone — and thus the person, for the phone is "almost a 'feature of human anatomy,'" *Carpenter*, 585 U.S. at 311 (quoting *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 385 (2014)) — wherever it sits, including the bedroom. Unlike the visual tailing of a car on public roads in *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), the ping does not stop at the curtilage; the officer at his desk has no idea, and need not care, whether the next fix will come from a highway or a home. Surveillance whose very design is indifferent to the home's threshold cannot be squared with *Karo*.

**4. Inescapability.** Cell phones are "indispensable to participation in modern society." *Carpenter*, 585 U.S. at 315. A rule permitting warrantless real-time pinging means that every American carrying a phone is subject to instantaneous government location at the unilateral discretion of any officer with a fax machine — including, as here, an officer pursuing a personal grudge against a co-worker who had filed a stalking complaint against him.

State courts of last resort confronting precisely this question have required a warrant. *Tracey v. State*, 152 So. 3d 504 (Fla. 2014); *Commonwealth v. Pacheco*, 263 A.3d 626 (Pa. 2021); *Almonor*, *supra*. The decision below makes the federal courts an outlier against the consistent judgment of the courts closest to these facts — including the supreme court of the very State where this surveillance occurred.

#### B. The decision below inverted this Court's reasoning.

The Thirteenth Circuit reasoned that because the tracking here gathered "less" than the weeks of data in *Jones* or the digital archive in *Riley*, it falls beneath some unstated floor of intrusion. That gets this Court's cases backwards. *Riley* and *Carpenter* did not establish a quantum of surveillance below which police may roam warrant-free; they recognized that digital surveillance differs in kind, not merely in degree, from its pre-digital analogues. The question is not whether Doakes could have gathered still more, but whether society recognizes as reasonable an expectation that the government will not secretly commandeer one's own pocket into reporting one's movements, fix after fix, for days. It does.

### II. THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE DOES NOT REACH THIS CASE, BECAUSE NOTHING WAS VOLUNTARILY CONVEYED — THE GOVERNMENT CREATED THE DATA IT SEIZED.

Even if *Miller* survives, it cannot sustain the judgment. The doctrine applies to information a person "voluntarily conveyed" to a third party and which the third party holds in the ordinary course of its business. *Miller*, 425 U.S. at 442; *Smith*, 442 U.S. at 744. Both predicates fail here.

#### A. No conveyance: Verizon acted as an agent of law enforcement, not a recipient of business records.

Miller wrote checks; Smith dialed numbers. Each performed an affirmative act transmitting discrete information to a business that recorded it for its own purposes, and the government later asked for the business's existing records. Petitioner did nothing of the kind. The location fixes at issue were not records Verizon kept in the ordinary course of providing service; they were manufactured on demand, when Verizon located the phone *because the government asked it to*. As *Almonor* recognized, such data "would not otherwise be collected and retained by the service provider." 120 N.E.3d 1183. When a carrier activates a device's location function solely to answer a police demand, it is not a third party at all — it is the government's instrument, and the Fourth Amendment applies to the government's agents as fully as to the government itself. Were it otherwise, the State could evade *Karo* by asking a private party to plant and monitor the beeper. A search warrant requirement that can be defeated by outsourcing the search is no requirement at all.

#### B. No voluntariness: Carpenter already exposed the fiction.

*Carpenter* held that cell-phone location data is not "shared" in any meaningful sense: a phone logs its position "without any affirmative act on the part of the user beyond powering up," and "in no meaningful sense does the user voluntarily 'assume the risk'" of turning over a comprehensive record of his movements. 585 U.S. at 315. If that is true of data the network generates passively, it is doubly true of data the user never generates at all — fixes conjured into existence by a government-directed ping the user cannot perceive, refuse, or avoid short of abandoning the device that is his ticket to modern life. The "assumption of risk" here is the assumption that one's own phone will not be turned into a tracking device by a stalker with a badge. No society that calls itself free treats that risk as voluntarily assumed.

### III. MILLER SHOULD BE OVERRULED: EVERY STARE DECISIS FACTOR SUPPORTS RETIRING THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE.

The Court granted certiorari to decide whether *Miller* should be overruled. It should be. *Stare decisis* is "at its weakest when we interpret the Constitution," because legislative correction is "practically impossible." *Payne*, 501 U.S. at 828. The Court considers the quality of the precedent's reasoning, its workability, its consistency with related decisions, changed factual circumstances, and reliance. *See Janus v. AFSCME*, 585 U.S. 878 (2018); *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83 (2020). Each factor points the same way.

**A. Quality of reasoning.** *Miller* rests on two errors. It equates secrecy with privacy — holding that disclosure to anyone, for any purpose, forfeits protection against everyone — a proposition *Katz* itself rejected when it protected words spoken into a telephone line necessarily carried by a third party. And it rests on a counterfeit "assumption of risk": no one assumes a risk he has no realistic ability to avoid. Privacy "is not an all-or-nothing proposition"; entrusting information to a bank or carrier for a limited purpose is not consent to its delivery to the police. *Smith*, 442 U.S. 735 (Marshall, J., dissenting). As Justice Sotomayor observed, the doctrine is "ill suited to the digital age, in which people reveal a great deal of information about themselves to third parties in the course of carrying out mundane tasks." *Jones*, 565 U.S. at 417 (Sotomayor, J., concurring).

**B. Unworkability.** *Carpenter* tried to save the doctrine by cabining it, and the result is a jurisprudence of unanswerable line-drawing. Seven days of historical CSLI is a search — but how many days are not? Historical data is covered, but real-time CSLI, tower dumps, IP logs, smart-home records, and search histories were all expressly reserved. 585 U.S. at 316. Lower courts — and, worse, officers in the field — are left to guess which categories of digitally intermediated life retain constitutional protection, and this very case proves they guess wrong. A doctrine that obliges this Court to announce a new technological exception every few years is not a workable rule; it is a slow-motion overruling. The Court should finish the job candidly: restore *Katz*'s ordinary reasonable-expectation inquiry, under which disclosure to a third party is one factor in the analysis, not a talisman that ends it.

**C. Doctrinal erosion.** *Miller* now stands in open tension with *Katz*, *Kyllo*, *Jones*, *Riley*, and above all *Carpenter*, which "decline[d] to extend" it precisely because its rationales collapse in the digital context. 585 U.S. at 314. A precedent already hollowed out by its own progeny has the weakest possible claim to preservation. *See Ramos*, 590 U.S. 83 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring) (inconsistency with related decisions supports overruling).

**D. Changed facts.** The 1976 depositor handed his bank a finite stack of paper. The 2026 citizen routes his correspondence, finances, movements, health, reading, and worship through third-party servers because there is no other way to live. A rule keyed to "what you share is unprotected" now means "almost nothing is protected." What may have been tolerable in 1976 is, as the dissent below put it, "profoundly out of date in 2026."

**E. Reliance.** The Government has no cognizable reliance interest in warrantless access to the people's private papers; the Fourth Amendment exists precisely to defeat that expectation. The warrant requirement is familiar, administrable, and already embedded in the SCA's own architecture, which makes warrants the default and preserves a true exigency pathway. Overruling *Miller* disarms no officer facing a genuine emergency — the exigent-circumstances exception survives untouched. It simply requires that the emergency be real. Here, by stipulation, it was not.

**F. This case is the refutation of the doctrine's promise.** Defenders of *Miller* assure the Court that statutes will police the gap. This case answers them. The SCA's emergency pathway demanded a genuine threat of death or serious injury. Doakes — weeks into an obsessive, unauthorized campaign of personal surveillance, the subject of a pending professional complaint by his target — simply checked "YES" on a fax form, and a continent-spanning carrier began reporting a citizen's location within the hour. No neutral magistrate ever stood between an angry man with a badge and the continuous whereabouts of his quarry, because under the decision below the Constitution did not require one. "[T]he Founders did not fight a revolution to gain the right to government agency protocols." *Riley*, 573 U.S. at 398. They fought it to put a judge between the executive and the people's private lives. At minimum, the Court should hold the third-party doctrine inapplicable to government-initiated, real-time location tracking; on the question it granted, it should overrule *Miller*.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Thirteenth Circuit should be reversed. The Court should hold that the warrantless real-time location surveillance of Petitioner was a Fourth Amendment search, overrule *Miller* and the third-party doctrine — or at minimum hold the doctrine inapplicable here — and remand for further proceedings.
