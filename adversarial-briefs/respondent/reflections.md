# Respondent's War-Room Log — Dexter Morgan v. United States

## Round 1

### (a) Theory of the case and strategic choices

Our theory: **Petitioner needs a revolution; we only need the status quo.** The question presented is framed at the maximum altitude — "overrule *Miller* and invalidate the third-party doctrine" — and that framing is our friend. We win if the Court does *any* of the following: (1) reaffirms *Miller*/*Smith* on stare decisis grounds; (2) holds these particular pings were not a search under *Knotts*/*Karo* without touching the doctrine; or (3) holds any search was reasonable under exigent circumstances. Petitioner must run the table; we hold three independent off-ramps.

Strategic choices made in the brief:

1. **Lead with stare decisis, not the facts.** The QP is about overruling precedent, so we put *Payne*/*Dobbs*/*Kimble* first and made Congress's reliance (the SCA itself) the centerpiece. The pitch: *Carpenter* already shows the Court can manage the digital age with a scalpel; overruling *Miller* is a wrecking ball that detonates grand-jury and subpoena practice across the entire economy. *Kimble*'s "enhanced force where Congress can fix it" point is the institutional heart of the argument.
2. **Recast *Carpenter* as proof of stability, not erosion.** Its express "narrow" self-limitation and explicit reservation of real-time CSLI (585 U.S. at 316) is the single most useful sentence in the U.S. Reports for us. We characterize *Carpenter*'s rationale as resting on three features — retrospectivity, comprehensiveness, indiscriminate accumulation — and show none is present in a handful of 30-minute-delayed prospective pings.
3. **Make the facts small.** "A swamp and a gas station." Two material data points, half-hour stale, on open land and a public highway. *Knotts* + open-fields (*Oliver*/*Hester*) does the doctrinal work; *Karo*'s home-interior limit confirms we're on the right side of the line. We repeatedly aggregate-minimize: "a handful of pings over a few days" vs. *Carpenter*'s 12,898 location points.
4. **Exigency as the constitutional backstop.** We argue the *statutory* stipulation (SCA emergency exception inapplicable) doesn't resolve the *constitutional* reasonableness question, citing *Virginia v. Moore* (state-law violation ≠ Fourth Amendment violation). An in-progress Butcher murder scene found Oct. 10, perpetrator fled and unlocated — objectively, that's *Warden v. Hayden* hot-pursuit/public-safety territory. We stress *Brigham City*'s objective test to neutralize Doakes's bad motives.
5. **Pre-rebut the dissent's three best points**: GPS-accuracy (record shows quarter-mile CSLI, coarser than the *Knotts* beeper); *Almonor* agency theory (tower registration is inherent to providing service); state cases (*Tracey*, *Pacheco*, *Almonor*) rest partly on state constitutions and can't dictate federal doctrine.
6. **Plant the no-suppression seed.** One sentence noting the SCA's remedies don't include suppression. Kept short — the QP isn't about remedy — but it gives the Court another face-saving exit.

### (b) Weaknesses I'm worried about

- **The stipulation is ugly for us.** Both parties agree the SCA's good-faith/emergency exception does *not* apply. Petitioner will say we're trying to smuggle the emergency back in through the Fourth Amendment after stipulating it away. Our *Moore* statutory/constitutional distinction is doctrinally sound, but it risks looking like gamesmanship, and a hostile Justice could read the stipulation broadly as conceding *no emergency existed at all* (in which case Part III collapses). I need a crisp answer to: "Counsel, didn't you stipulate there was no emergency?" Best answer so far: the stipulation concerns the *statutory* standard (the provider's good-faith belief at the time of the Oct. 8 fax) — it cannot retroactively erase the objective facts of Oct. 10, and constitutional reasonableness is measured objectively on the totality.
- **Doakes's conduct is indefensible.** He was *extrajudicially stalking* Morgan for weeks, lost his target out of "frustration," and falsely certified an emergency on October 8 — *two days before* the in-progress crime scene was found. At the moment of certification there was no specific exigency, only a general manhunt. My exigency timeline has a two-day gap I papered over by focusing on the Oct. 10 pings (the only ones that mattered evidentiarily). Petitioner will hammer the false certification as exactly the "arbitrary invasion" the Fourth Amendment exists to prevent, and as Exhibit A for why the third-party doctrine invites abuse: any rogue officer can fax a form and track an enemy.
- **The "voluntary conveyance" fiction.** *Carpenter* itself said cell phone location data is not "shared" in any meaningful voluntary sense — carrying a phone is "indispensable to participation in modern society" and the data is generated "without any affirmative act." If voluntariness fails for historical CSLI, it's hard to say it succeeds for real-time CSLI from the same phone. My answer (tower registration is inherent to service; *Knotts* makes the *public* character of the movements dispositive regardless) works but is in tension: I'm simultaneously arguing third-party doctrine applies *and* that I don't need it because of *Knotts*. A sharp opponent will expose that hedge.
- **Pinged vs. passively observed.** If the carrier *actively pinged* the handset (activated GPS at police request) rather than reporting routine tower registrations, the *Almonor* agency argument gets real traction and the third-party doctrine's "records held for business purposes" rationale weakens. The record is ambiguous ("pinged," "GPS data provided by Verizon" in the suppression motion, but "cell-site data" at trial and quarter-mile accuracy). I exploited the ambiguity toward CSLI; Petitioner will exploit it toward GPS.
- **Several days of tracking, not two pings.** Doakes received location data "for the next several days" (Oct. 8–10+). I foregrounded the two material pings, but the *collection* was continuous-ish over multiple days. If the Court counts the full stream, the gap between this and *Carpenter*'s "seven days" shrinks. The Fifth Circuit and others have rejected mosaic line-drawing below seven days, but the optics worsen.
- **Stare decisis after *Dobbs* cuts both ways.** Citing *Dobbs* for retention of precedent is awkward; Petitioner will cite it for the proposition that egregiously wrong, unworkable precedents should fall, and will quote Gorsuch's *Carpenter* dissent calling third-party doctrine's rationale into question, plus Sotomayor's *Jones* concurrence.

### (c) Predicted Petitioner arguments

1. *Miller*'s "assumption of risk" rationale was always thin (Marshall/Brennan dissents) and is incoherent in a world where third-party data sharing is involuntary and ubiquitous — quoting Sotomayor's *Jones* concurrence and *Carpenter*'s own anti-voluntariness language.
2. *Carpenter* already did the doctrinal demolition work; overruling *Miller* just acknowledges reality. Real-time tracking is *worse* than historical: it turns the phone into a live ankle monitor, enabling pursuit, interception, and (as here) harassment.
3. The facts as parade-of-horribles: a stalker with a badge, a false emergency certification, no warrant, no statutory safe harbor (stipulated), tracking his personal enemy for days. If the third-party doctrine permits this, the doctrine is the problem.
4. Workability inversion: *Carpenter*-plus-exceptions is the unworkable regime; a clean warrant rule for location data is simple. Warrants are fast (telephonic/electronic), and genuine emergencies are covered by exigency doctrine — which the Government *cannot* invoke here because of the stipulation (they'll argue waiver).
5. *Tracey* (Florida — the very state!), *Pacheco*, *Almonor*, and federal district decisions treating real-time pinging as a search; the agency theory that pinging creates data the carrier never had, taking it outside any "business records" rationale.
6. Possibly a *Jones*-style trespass-to-chattels theory (commandeering the phone's radio to report location = physical intrusion on an effect), echoing Gorsuch's property-based approach.

### (d) Open questions for the brainstormer

1. **How hard should we lean on Part III (exigency) given the stipulation?** Is there a cleaner way to frame the statutory/constitutional distinction so it doesn't smell like end-running a stipulation? Should we add an inevitable-discovery / attenuation / good-faith (Leon/Davis-style) fallback for Round 2, or does that dilute focus?
2. **GPS vs. CSLI record ambiguity** — embrace it or resolve it? Should Round 2 affirmatively argue the quarter-mile accuracy concession at trial *judicially defines* the data as coarse CSLI?
3. **Doakes taint**: is there a respectable standing/causation move — the *evidence* used at trial (Scuteri's maps) was derived from carrier records that could have been obtained with the warrant the FBI plainly had probable cause for by Oct. 10 — or is independent-source too factbound for this posture?
4. **Suppression remedy argument**: courts (e.g., under the SCA) uniformly hold the statute provides no suppression remedy. Should this be promoted from one sentence to a full alternative ground ("even if a search, exclusion is not the remedy for a statutory-process violation"), or is that outside the QP?
5. **How to handle Gorsuch's *Carpenter* dissent** — preempt in Round 2 or wait? His property framing arguably *helps* us (no positive-law property interest in Verizon's records under the SCA's disclosure provisions?).
6. **Tone check**: the "swamp and a gas station" minimization is rhetorically strong, but does the "several days of pings" fact make it vulnerable as a misdescription? Consider recalibrating to "two evidentially material fixes out of a brief, targeted collection."

## Round 2

### (a) Changes made

1. **Added a Statement of Facts.** Round 1 argued facts inside argument headings; the brainstormer flagged it and Petitioner's narrative ("a stalker with a badge") was beating ours. The new Statement opens with the eighteen bodies and the insider-killer finding, places Doakes in one candid sentence ("acting on his own . . . the Government does not defend Doakes's certification"), and ends on the two record facts we most need the Court to retain: the rangers' *independent* discovery of the crime scene, and the quarter-mile trial testimony as "the only quantified measure of accuracy in this record."
2. **Killed the minimization.** Excised "a handful of delayed location pings" from the QP and "a handful of discrete pings" from the argument. The brief now concedes outright: "The Government does not pretend this was a single ping; Doakes received fixes over several days." Replaced with the honest framing the brainstormer recommended: a brief, targeted stream of which only the two October 10 fixes were material — and even counted whole, it sits within *Carpenter* footnote 3's reservation. Also cut the unsupported "coarser fix than the *Knotts* beeper itself provided" line.
3. **Fixed *Kimble*.** Round 1 cited *Kimble*'s "enhanced force" language for a constitutional precedent — a genuine misapplication Petitioner would have turned into a credibility wound. *Kimble* now appears only for the generic special-justification standard; the institutional point is reframed as legislative competence (Congress can layer protection above the constitutional floor) without invoking any heightened stare decisis tier. I also now *concede* Payne's constitutional-weakness point and answer it ("weakest is not nonexistent") rather than ignoring it.
4. **New stare decisis attack line: the missing replacement rule.** Per the brainstormer's overall assessment, Petitioner's Round 1 never says what governs a grand-jury subpoena for brokerage records after Miller falls. Section I.B now demands the successor rule explicitly ("Petitioner offers a mood") and cites *Oklahoma Press* to show what his regime would destabilize. Also added a direct answer to his "no legitimate reliance in unconstitutional practices" move: it's "the conclusion dressed as a premise," and the operative reliance is legislative (the SCA), not police convenience.
5. **Rebuilt Part II around the a-fortiori rebuttal and federal authority.** New II.A argues real-time is not historical-plus: *Carpenter*'s mechanism of harm was the retrospective archive ("travel back in time"), and a prospective targeted fix replicates *Knotts* tailing, which is why the *Carpenter* reservation exists at all ("the reservation would have been pointless if real-time tracking were covered a fortiori"). Added *United States v. Hammond*, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021), and *United States v. Riley*, 858 F.3d 1012 (6th Cir. 2017) — federal circuit holdings that short-term real-time CSLI tracking of public movements is not a search — so we are no longer merely distinguishing Petitioner's state cases; we now have affirmative federal authority he lacks.
6. **Answered *Almonor* head-on (II.C) instead of hand-waving.** Three-step rebuttal: (i) the record shows tower-scale data (quarter-mile; "GPS data" is the label in Petitioner's own motion, not a finding) — adopting the brainstormer's advice to resolve the dataset ambiguity affirmatively; (ii) the agency theory proves too little — the *Knotts* beeper was itself a government-installed device with *no* voluntary conveyance, and monitoring it on public roads still wasn't a search, so agency vel non cannot be dispositive where what's revealed is public movement (this is, I think, the strongest new analytical move in the brief); (iii) expressly demoted voluntariness to a secondary theory, resolving the Round 1 hedge the brainstormer said Petitioner would expose.
7. **Untangled the two datasets.** Per the Shared Notes: the trial maps were historical cell-site records, a distinct sub-seven-day dataset within footnote 3 — used both in II.A (no search) and IV (independent of the real-time stream).
8. **Restructured exigency (III) as honest severability.** The argument now concedes the Court "may assume the October 8–9 stream was unlawful" and defends only the October 10 acquisitions, which coincided with the rangers' in-progress-murder discovery. Preempted the freeze-out attack (exigency is objective and it was Lundy's FBI that acted) and distinguished *Mincey* ourselves (home-search murder-scene exception vs. delayed location fixes) before Petitioner could turn our own Round 1 cite against us — the brainstormer told them to.
9. **Promoted no-suppression to a full Part IV.** Three grounds: SCA remedial exclusivity (§§ 2707–2708; *Guerrero*), *Herring*/*Davis* cost-benefit with the "doctrine should not be redesigned around its worst user" framing, and independent source/harmless error (*Murray*/*Nix* — the rangers found the scene first). Folded the Gorsuch-preempt into one clause: a positive-law interest comes with positive-law remedies. Closing line — "at the very most . . . a remand, not the overruling of *Miller*" — gives the Court its cheapest exit.
10. **Preempted Rule 41(b)(4)** (II.D): it governs government-installed devices under § 3117; Congress put carrier-derived records in the SCA with different process — the structure cuts our way. The brainstormer handed Petitioner this argument; better to be holding the answer before it lands.
11. **Citation hygiene:** 18 U.S.C. § 2702 (not the problem's "17 U.S.C." typo); dropped the unverified "everyone, all the time" quotation in favor of paraphrase ("automatically as to every subscriber").

### (b) Responding to the opposition

What worried me most, in order:

- **The Almonor agency/no-conveyance theory.** The brainstormer called it the single strongest analytical move in either brief, and it was — our Round 1 tower-registration answer didn't engage on-demand pinging. The *Knotts*-beeper-was-government-installed point is my answer: it shows that even *zero* conveyance doesn't make public-movement monitoring a search, so the agency theory attacks a doctrine we don't need for affirmance. Paired with the record point (quarter-mile = tower data; no GPS activation shown), I think the theory is now contained rather than conceded.
- **The duration/record-shading attack** ("The Government will call this a single-ping case. The record refutes that."). They had already loaded that gun in Round 1. The only defense was to concede the several days openly and convert it into severability/materiality — which also strengthened Part III.
- **The a-fortiori Carpenter argument.** Rhetorically powerful ("live homing beacon"). My answer is that it mistakes *Carpenter*'s mechanism: the archive, not the location datum, was the harm. The footnote-pointlessness argument (why reserve real-time if it's covered a fortiori?) is the crispest version.
- **Their Karo "indifferent to the threshold" move.** Answered with Karo's own actual-revelation holding — capability tests are not the law, and no fix touched a protected space.
- **The parade of Doakes.** Unanswerable on its own terms, so I stopped trying to outrun it: the Statement concedes it, Part IV channels it (§ 2707, discipline, prosecution; the rule must be written for the honest officer).

### (d) Remaining vulnerabilities and questions for the brainstormer

1. **The Hammond/Riley descriptions.** I am confident both cases exist and held real-time tracking of public movements not a search (Hammond also had an exigency alternative). If my parentheticals overstate their holdings even slightly, Petitioner's Round 3 will feast. Please verify the characterizations.
2. **The "query of the carrier's own operational data" move** (II.C, first answer) is my weakest link: if the Court believes a ping *commands* the network to locate the handset rather than report what it already knows, the distinction blurs. Is there a cleaner formulation, or should I retreat fully to the Knotts-beeper point?
3. **§ 2708 "exclusivity."** I cite § 2708 for remedial exclusivity. That is the standard reading in the no-suppression cases, but if the brainstormer thinks the characterization is contestable I'd rather hedge to "Congress provided civil remedies and omitted suppression."
4. **Severability concession risk.** Part III now assumes arguendo that Oct. 8–9 was unlawful. If the Court treats the stream as one indivisible "search" that began Oct. 8, the concession could bleed into Part II's no-search holding. I think the discrete-acquisition framing holds, but I'd like the brainstormer's read.
5. **Expected Round 3 moves:** the Kerr-style limited-overruling rule (records that are the product of protected activity vs. the business's own ledgers) — which would blunt my "no replacement rule" attack; the "Carpenter already amputated § 2703(d) and the sky didn't fall" reliance rebuttal; and a fruits argument that the trial maps were derived from the tainted real-time stream. For the last, my dataset-distinction is only as strong as the record's silence — is there a better anchor than "cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier"?
6. **Tone check:** Is "Petitioner offers a mood" a line too far for an SG brief? It's the one flourish I kept; happy to trade it for credibility if it reads as snark.
