## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should discard fifty years of settled Fourth Amendment doctrine by overruling *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and abolishing the third-party doctrine, where law enforcement — in the midst of an active manhunt for the deadliest serial killer in American history — obtained from a wireless carrier a handful of delayed location pings showing only that Petitioner's phone moved from a remote swamp to a service station on a public highway.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

Petitioner asks this Court to do what it has pointedly and repeatedly declined to do: raze the third-party doctrine root and branch. Every consideration of stare decisis counsels against that request. *Miller* and *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979), have governed for half a century. They are workable, they are woven into the fabric of federal and state investigative law, and Congress has legislated an entire statutory architecture — the Stored Communications Act — in reliance on them. *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), did not erode that foundation; it expressly preserved it, carving a "narrow" exception for a qualitatively unique category of evidence: a retrospective, comprehensive, week-long archive of a person's every movement. This Court declined to extend that exception to real-time CSLI in *Carpenter* itself, and nothing in this case warrants going further.

Nor does Petitioner's case present a vehicle for revolution. What the Government obtained here was not a digital dossier but a few attenuated data points, delayed by thirty minutes, showing a cell phone in the open expanse of the Everglades and then at a gas station on Florida State Road 9336. Under *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), a person traveling on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another. The pings here revealed precisely that — and nothing more. They did not penetrate a home, *United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984); they did not expose the "privacies of life," *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014); and they did not assemble the kind of all-encompassing historical chronicle that drove the result in *Carpenter*.

Finally, even if the Court were to deem this limited collection a "search," it was a reasonable one. The Fourth Amendment forbids unreasonable searches, not warrantless ones, and the exigencies of an ongoing serial-killer investigation — confirmed when park rangers stumbled upon an in-progress murder scene matching the Bay Harbor Butcher's signature — place this case comfortably within the heartland of exigent-circumstances doctrine. The parties' stipulation that the SCA's *statutory* emergency provision is unavailable does not and cannot resolve the distinct *constitutional* question of reasonableness. *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008).

The judgment below should be affirmed.

## ARGUMENT

### I. STARE DECISIS FORECLOSES OVERRULING *MILLER* AND THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE.

#### A. The third-party doctrine rests on a sound and enduring principle of voluntary exposure.

The third-party doctrine is not an arbitrary relic. It flows from the first principles of *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967): "[w]hat a person knowingly exposes to the public . . . is not a subject of Fourth Amendment protection." *Id.* at 351. *Miller* applied that principle to records a customer voluntarily conveyed to his bank; *Smith* applied it to numbers a caller voluntarily transmitted to his phone company. The animating insight — that one who reveals information to another assumes the risk it will be conveyed to the Government — long predates the digital age and extends well beyond it. *See United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971); *Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293 (1966); *California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988). The doctrine reflects a structural truth about the Fourth Amendment: it protects *the people's* papers and effects, not business records created and held by someone else for that party's own purposes.

#### B. Every traditional stare decisis factor favors retention.

Overruling precedent demands "special justification," not merely a belief that a case was wrongly decided. *Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC*, 576 U.S. 446 (2015). The factors this Court has identified — the quality of the decision's reasoning, its workability, its consistency with related decisions, and reliance interests, *see Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808 (1991); *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022) — all point the same direction here.

**Workability.** For fifty years, the third-party doctrine has supplied a clear, administrable line that police officers, magistrates, and providers can apply in the field. Petitioner's proposed regime — case-by-case judicial assessment of the "sensitivity" of every category of record held by every third party — is the opposite of workable. It would convert every subpoena for bank records, toll records, shipping manifests, or utility bills into a constitutional litigation event.

**Reliance.** The reliance interests here are extraordinary, and they are not merely governmental. Congress legislated *against the backdrop of Miller and Smith* when it enacted the Stored Communications Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2701–2712 — a reticulated scheme calibrating, category by category, what process is required for what records, including an emergency-disclosure mechanism, § 2702. Grand jury practice, administrative subpoena authority, and routine securities, tax, and fraud enforcement are all built on the premise that compelled production of third-party business records is not a Fourth Amendment search. Petitioner does not ask the Court to trim a doctrine; he asks it to detonate the foundation under decades of congressional architecture.

**No doctrinal erosion.** *Carpenter* is not a crack in the foundation; it is proof of the foundation's stability. The Court there went out of its way to say that its decision "is a narrow one" and that it did "not disturb the application of *Smith* and *Miller*." 585 U.S. at 316. Where this Court has so recently reaffirmed a precedent's continuing vitality, the case for overruling it is at its nadir.

#### C. Constitutionalizing this field would displace Congress, the institution best suited to regulate it.

Petitioner's real grievance is with the pace of statutory privacy law, and his remedy lies across the street. Congress has shown itself ready and able to legislate in this space — the SCA itself is the proof — and statutory rules can be recalibrated as technology changes in ways that constitutional rulings cannot. *Kimble* teaches that stare decisis carries "enhanced force" where, as here, the political branches can correct any error. 576 U.S. at 456. A decision constitutionalizing all third-party records would freeze into the Fourth Amendment a one-size-fits-all warrant rule that no legislature could adjust, displacing the nuanced, democratically accountable regime Congress has already built.

### II. THE LIMITED, DELAYED LOCATION PINGS OBTAINED HERE WERE NOT A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. *Carpenter* is a deliberately narrow exception, and its rationale does not reach this case.

*Carpenter* rested on the unique character of *historical* CSLI: a "detailed, encyclopedic, and effortlessly compiled" record, retrospective in nature, that gave police access to a comprehensive chronicle reaching back as far as the carrier's retention policies allowed — in effect, "near perfect surveillance" of the past. 585 U.S. at 309–312. Three features did the work: (1) the *retrospective* quality of the data, which let police "travel back in time" to retrace a person's whereabouts; (2) its *comprehensiveness*, cataloguing every movement, into every private space, over an extended period; and (3) its *automatic, indiscriminate* accumulation as to "everyone, all the time," without any forward-looking investigative predicate.

None of those features is present here. Doakes obtained no archive of Petitioner's past. He received a *handful* of discrete, prospective pings over a few days, each delayed by thirty minutes — data that could never reconstruct where Petitioner had been before October 8, and that in fact revealed only two material facts: the phone was in a remote stretch of the Everglades, and then at a service station ten miles away on a public road. The Court in *Carpenter* expressly withheld judgment on "real-time CSLI," *id.* at 316, precisely because the retrospective-archive rationale does not translate. A few forward-looking pings are different in kind, not merely in degree, from seven days of historical reconstruction — and they fall on the *Knotts* side of the line, not the *Carpenter* side.

#### B. *Knotts* controls: there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in movements on public thoroughfares and open lands.

In *Knotts*, this Court held that monitoring a beeper's signals as a vehicle traveled public roads "did not invade any legitimate expectation of privacy," because a "person traveling in an automobile on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another." 460 U.S. at 281–282. The pings here did exactly what the beeper in *Knotts* did — and less. They located the phone in the open Everglades (terrain that is the very archetype of an "open field" in which no reasonable expectation of privacy exists, *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984); *Hester v. United States*, 265 U.S. 57 (1924)) and at a public service station on Florida State Road 9336, from which Doakes inferred a direction of travel any motorist on that highway could have observed.

The limiting principle of *Karo* — that location monitoring becomes a search when it reveals information about the *interior of a private residence* not otherwise observable — confirms the result. 468 U.S. at 714–716. No ping placed Petitioner's phone in his home or any other constitutionally protected enclave. And *Jones* is doubly inapposite: there was no physical trespass on Petitioner's property, 565 U.S. at 404–405, and no "month-long" mosaic of movements of the sort that troubled the concurring Justices. *Riley*, which concerned the vast *contents* of a smartphone — photographs, messages, browsing history, "the privacies of life," 573 U.S. at 403 — is further afield still. The few coordinates obtained here revealed no familial, political, religious, or medical association. They revealed a swamp and a gas station.

#### C. Petitioner's rule has no limiting principle.

If a thirty-minute-delayed ping showing a phone on a public highway is a constitutional "search" requiring a warrant, then so is every fugitive-location request, every effort to find an abducted child's phone, and every attempt to locate an armed suspect in flight. Courts applying *Carpenter* have consistently recognized that *short-term, targeted* location checks do not implicate the decision's concerns. The line *Carpenter* itself drew — extended historical compilation on one side, limited observation of public movement on the other — is administrable and faithful to *Katz*. Petitioner's line is no line at all.

### III. EVEN IF A SEARCH OCCURRED, IT WAS REASONABLE WITHOUT A WARRANT GIVEN THE EXIGENCIES OF AN ACTIVE SERIAL-KILLER MANHUNT.

"[T]he ultimate touchstone of the Fourth Amendment is 'reasonableness.'" *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 403 (2006). The warrant requirement yields where "the exigencies of the situation make the needs of law enforcement so compelling that a warrantless search is objectively reasonable." *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452, 460 (2011); *see Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967); *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385, 392–394 (1978) (recognizing the need to act where there is reason to believe a life is in danger); *Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141 (2013) (exigency assessed case by case under the totality of the circumstances).

The objective circumstances here were as compelling as they come. The deadliest serial killer in American history — eighteen confirmed victims — was at large, active, and operating in Miami. On October 10, park rangers interrupted a murder and cover-up *in progress*, forensically matched to the Butcher's signature; the perpetrator had fled and remained unlocated. Within hours, the very pings at issue placed Petitioner's phone in the remote Everglades — where the fresh crime scene and the body of Hector Estrada were then discovered — and tracked his flight along the public highway toward Miami. Whatever may be said of Doakes's subjective motives, the Fourth Amendment inquiry is objective, *Brigham City*, 547 U.S. at 404, and an objectively reasonable officer confronting an active, escalating string of homicides could conclude that immediate location information was necessary to prevent the next killing and apprehend a fleeing murderer.

The parties' stipulation that the *statutory* good-faith/emergency exception of 18 U.S.C. § 2702 is unavailable does not answer this question. Statutory compliance and constitutional reasonableness are distinct inquiries; a violation of statute neither establishes nor forecloses a Fourth Amendment violation. *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164, 176–178 (2008). If Congress's scheme was transgressed, the remedy lies in the SCA's own enforcement provisions — which conspicuously do not include suppression — not in a constitutional exclusionary rule, and certainly not in the demolition of *Miller*.

## CONCLUSION

For half a century, *Miller* and *Smith* have supplied a stable, workable framework on which Congress, law enforcement, and the lower courts have built. *Carpenter* shows this Court can police the digital frontier with a scalpel; Petitioner demands a wrecking ball. The Court should decline the invitation, reaffirm *United States v. Miller* and the third-party doctrine, hold that the limited delayed pings obtained here were not a Fourth Amendment search — or, at minimum, that no warrant was required in the exigent circumstances of this manhunt — and affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Thirteenth Circuit.
