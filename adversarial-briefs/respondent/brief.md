## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should discard fifty years of settled Fourth Amendment doctrine by overruling *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and abolishing the third-party doctrine, where law enforcement — during an active manhunt for the deadliest serial killer in American history — obtained from a wireless carrier a brief, targeted stream of delayed location fixes, the only material two of which showed Petitioner's phone in the open Everglades and at a service station on a public highway.

## STATEMENT

Eighteen bodies lay on the floor of Bay Harbor, each the work of a single killer. By October 2007 the FBI had assumed control of the largest investigation in Miami's history, and the evidence indicated that the Bay Harbor Butcher was a law-enforcement insider still at large. On October 8, Detective James Doakes — acting on his own — faxed Verizon an emergency-disclosure form under 18 U.S.C. § 2702 requesting "current" location information for Petitioner's phone. The parties have stipulated that the statutory emergency exception does not apply, and the Government does not defend Doakes's certification. For the next several days, Verizon furnished location data, each fix delayed by thirty minutes.

On October 10, park rangers stumbled upon a murder and cover-up *in progress* in the Everglades, forensically matched to the Butcher's signature; the perpetrator had fled and was unlocated. That same day, two fixes showed Petitioner's phone in a remote part of the Everglades and then at a service station ten miles away on Florida State Road 9336. FBI agents — to whom Doakes relayed the information — located Petitioner at a Miami drive-thru and arrested him; a search of his trunk revealed knives, plastic wrap, and luminol, and agents recovered the body of Hector Estrada at the scene the rangers had already discovered. At trial, the Government's expert mapped "cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier," placing the phone "within a quarter mile" of the murder scene — the only quantified measure of accuracy in this record.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

This case arises not from idle surveillance but from the hunt for an active serial killer who, on the very day of the material location fixes, had been interrupted mid-murder and had vanished into the Everglades. Petitioner asks the Court to answer that record with a constitutional revolution: overrule *Miller*, abolish the third-party doctrine, and constitutionalize every subpoena for third-party records in the American economy.

Stare decisis forecloses that request. *Miller* and *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979), have governed for half a century; Congress built the Stored Communications Act on their foundation; and this Court reaffirmed them — expressly — only eight years ago in *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296, 316 (2018). Petitioner's new successor rule — protection for records that "chronicle" the customer's activities, but not for a business's own "ledgers" — dissolves on contact with actual records, which are almost always both at once; cannot decide even *Miller* itself, whose outcome Petitioner concedes "could survive"; and to the extent it has content, replicates what *Carpenter* already provides. A rule that cannot decide the case it replaces is a reason to retain the framework, not raze it.

Nor was the acquisition here a search. *Carpenter*'s holding rested on the retrospective, encyclopedic character of a week-long historical archive; the Court expressly reserved real-time CSLI, and the federal courts of appeals applying *Carpenter* have held that short-term, targeted real-time locating of a phone moving through public space is not a search. *United States v. Hammond*, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021); *United States v. Riley*, 858 F.3d 1012 (6th Cir. 2017). That conclusion follows from *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), and does not depend on the third-party doctrine at all: the fixes here revealed movements in open wetlands and on a public highway, nothing more.

Finally, even if a search occurred, the October 10 acquisitions — the only ones that mattered — were objectively reasonable under the exigent circumstances of an interrupted homicide, and suppression would in any event be an improper remedy for what is, at bottom, a statutory-process violation by a rogue officer whom Congress has given his victims other means to punish.

The judgment below should be affirmed.

## ARGUMENT

### I. STARE DECISIS FORECLOSES OVERRULING *MILLER* AND THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE.

#### A. The doctrine rests on a sound and enduring principle.

The third-party doctrine flows from *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967): what a person "knowingly exposes" to others is not protected. *Miller* applied that principle to records a customer conveyed to his bank; *Smith* to numbers dialed to the phone company. *See also United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971); *California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988). The Fourth Amendment protects *the people's* papers and effects — not business records created and held by another for its own purposes.

#### B. Petitioner has no special justification — and his proposed successor rule is no rule at all.

Overruling precedent requires "special justification," not a present belief that the case was wrongly decided. *Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC*, 576 U.S. 446, 455 (2015). Petitioner answers that constitutional stare decisis is at its weakest, *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991) — true, but weakest is not nonexistent, and every factor this Court actually weighs, *see Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022), cuts against him.

**Recent reaffirmation.** *Carpenter* is not erosion; it is reaffirmation. The Court considered these very criticisms of *Miller* and *Smith*, declined to "disturb" either, and called its own holding "a narrow one." 585 U.S. at 316. Petitioner's claim that *Carpenter* "hollowed out" *Miller* inverts what the Court said it was doing eight years ago.

**Workability.** Petitioner now offers a successor rule: the Fourth Amendment protects third-party records that "chronicle the customer's own protected activities," while a business's "own transactional ledgers" remain subject to ordinary subpoena reasonableness under *Oklahoma Press Publishing Co. v. Walling*, 327 U.S. 186 (1946). The rule fails at its seams. *First*, the categories are not categories: nearly every consumer record is both at once. A credit-card statement is the issuer's ledger of its receivables *and* a chronicle of the customer's travels, purchases, and associations — and so were *Miller*'s own bank records, which is precisely why prosecutors wanted them. *Second*, the rule cannot decide even its founding case: Petitioner concedes that under his test "*Miller*'s outcome could survive." An overruling that leaves the overruled case's result intact is not a workable successor; it is an instruction to relitigate every subpoena — the case-by-case regime Petitioner condemns. *Third*, whatever work the chronicle concept can do, *Carpenter* already does, protecting comprehensive movement records while leaving "conventional surveillance techniques" and ordinary "business records" untouched. 585 U.S. at 316. A replacement that replicates existing doctrine is the opposite of a special justification for discarding it.

**Reliance.** Congress legislated against *Miller* and *Smith* when it enacted the SCA, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2701–2712, calibrating process category by category. Petitioner says the Government has "no legitimate reliance" in unconstitutional practices — but that is the conclusion dressed as a premise. The reliance here is *legislative*: an entire statutory architecture, plus decades of tax, fraud, and securities enforcement, built on the settled understanding that compelled production of third-party business records is not a search. Petitioner's reply — that *Carpenter* already displaced § 2703(d) for one record category "without incident" — proves the Government's point: a targeted, category-specific adjustment left the architecture standing. Wholesale overruling removes its foundation.

#### C. Calibration belongs to Congress.

Statutes can be recalibrated as technology changes; constitutional rulings cannot. Congress remains free to layer protections above the constitutional floor, as it did in the SCA itself. A decision constitutionalizing all third-party records would freeze a one-size-fits-all warrant rule into the Amendment, displacing a nuanced, democratically accountable regime. *Carpenter* shows the Court can police genuine digital-age excesses with a scalpel; Petitioner demands a wrecking ball.

### II. THE LIMITED REAL-TIME ACQUISITION HERE WAS NOT A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. *Carpenter*'s rationale does not extend to short-term, targeted, prospective locating — and Petitioner's *a fortiori* argument inverts it.

Petitioner argues that real-time tracking is *worse* than historical CSLI, so *Carpenter* governs *a fortiori*. That misreads what did the work in *Carpenter*. The Court's concern was the *retrospective archive*: a "detailed, encyclopedic, and effortlessly compiled" record letting police "travel back in time to retrace a person's whereabouts," compiled automatically as to every subscriber without any investigative predicate. 585 U.S. at 309–312. A prospective, targeted location fix has none of those features. It cannot reconstruct the past; it reaches only one suspect, upon a specific request; and it replicates what *Knotts* has always permitted — following a particular suspect's present movements — rather than conferring the time-machine omniscience that troubled the *Carpenter* Court. That is why *Carpenter* expressly declined to express a view on "real-time CSLI," *id.* at 316: the rationale does not translate, and the reservation would have been pointless if real-time tracking were covered *a fortiori*.

The federal courts of appeals agree. In *Hammond*, the Seventh Circuit held post-*Carpenter* that real-time CSLI used to locate a suspect on public highways was not a search, because *Knotts*, not *Carpenter*, supplies the rule for tracking present public movements. *Riley* reached the same conclusion for roughly seven hours of real-time tracking of a fugitive that revealed nothing about the interior of any protected space. The Government does not oversell either case: *Hammond* rested alternatively on exigent circumstances — an overlay mirroring this record's October 10, *see* Part III, *infra* — and *Riley* preceded *Carpenter*. But *Riley*'s operative analysis is *Karo*'s, which *Carpenter* left untouched, and it found no search even for phone-GPS-grade data more precise than the tower-scale fixes here. Nor does duration distinguish either case: those locates ran hours and this one days, but footnote 3 reserved the entire real-time category without blessing any period, and no fix here, early or late, touched a protected space. Petitioner's contrary authorities — *Tracey*, *Pacheco*, *Almonor* — are state decisions resting in substantial part on state constitutional guarantees; on the federal question, the federal authority is against him.

As for this record's duration: the Government does not pretend this was a single ping — Doakes received fixes over several days. But the stream was a sequence of discrete, thirty-minute-stale fixes on one suspect, and the only two that mattered showed the phone in open wetlands and at a service station on a public road. Even counted whole, it sits within the territory *Carpenter* reserved, *see id.* at 310 n.3, far from the seven-day historical archive it condemned.

#### B. *Knotts* resolves this case without any help from *Miller*.

Even if the Court narrowed or retired the third-party doctrine, the judgment stands, because "[a] person traveling in an automobile on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another." *Knotts*, 460 U.S. at 281. The fixes located the phone in the Everglades — the archetypal open field, *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984) — and at a public service station, from which Doakes inferred a direction of travel visible to any motorist.

*United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984), marks the line, and confirms which side this case falls on: monitoring becomes a search when it *actually reveals* information about the interior of a private residence "that could not have been obtained by observation from outside," *id.* at 715. Petitioner's answer — that real-time pinging is "indifferent to the home's threshold" — converts *Karo*'s actual-revelation holding into a capability test this Court has never adopted. No fix here placed the phone in a home or any protected enclave. Nor does *Knotts*' reservation of "dragnet-type" practices — twenty-four-hour surveillance of any citizen — assist Petitioner: a days-long series of half-hour-stale fixes on one identified suspect in an active manhunt is targeted investigation, not a dragnet. The open-ended "current" box on Doakes's form measures the request's sloppiness, not the search's scope; the Fourth Amendment judges what the Government actually acquired, not what a form might have permitted.

#### C. The *Almonor* agency theory fails on this record and under this Court's cases.

Petitioner's strongest analytical move is that nothing was "voluntarily conveyed" because Verizon, at police command, *generated* the location data — acting as an agent, not a records custodian. Three answers.

*First*, the record does not show GPS activation. The only quantified accuracy in this record is the trial expert's "within a quarter mile" — tower-scale, not GPS-scale — and "GPS data" appears only as the label in Petitioner's own suppression motion; what the carrier provided was "cell-site data." A network necessarily knows which of its towers serves each powered handset; reporting that operational fact on request is not the manufacture of new information.

*Second*, the agency theory proves too little. The *Knotts* beeper was itself a *government-installed* device — no voluntary conveyance whatsoever — and its monitoring on public roads still was not a search. The dispositive question under *Katz* is what the surveillance *revealed*, not the corporate identity of who relayed it; where, as here, what was revealed is public movement, the conveyance debate is academic.

*Third*, voluntariness is in any event secondary: the Government's lead submission is *Knotts*, and the Court need not resolve how *Carpenter*'s voluntariness discussion, 585 U.S. at 315, maps onto prospective fixes in order to affirm.

#### D. Petitioner's rule has no limiting principle, and the statutory structure cuts against him.

If a thirty-minute-stale fix on a public highway is a warrant-only event, so is every effort to locate an abducted child's phone or an armed fugitive. Petitioner's structural argument — that Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(b)(4) treats prospective tracking as warrant territory — proves the opposite: that rule governs *government-installed tracking devices*, 18 U.S.C. § 3117. Congress addressed carrier-derived location information separately, in the SCA, with calibrated lesser process and an emergency pathway expressly contemplating immediate disclosure of *current* information, § 2702(b)(8), (c)(4) — refuting the claim that the statute "was never a charter" for prospective disclosures. Congress itself assigned this category to the statutory, not constitutional, domain.

### III. ALTERNATIVELY, THE OCTOBER 10 ACQUISITIONS WERE REASONABLE UNDER THE EXIGENT-CIRCUMSTANCES DOCTRINE.

The reasonableness inquiry attaches to each governmental acquisition, and the only fixes that located Petitioner or figured in this prosecution were obtained on October 10. The Government does not defend Doakes's October 8 certification, and the Court may assume the October 8–9 stream was unlawful. But by October 10 the objective picture had transformed: rangers had interrupted a Butcher murder *in progress* that day; the killer had fled and was unlocated; eighteen prior victims attested to what delay would cost. An officer may act without a warrant where "the exigencies of the situation" make it objectively reasonable, *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452, 460 (2011); *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967), and the inquiry is objective — Doakes's motives are irrelevant, *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 404 (2006). The FBI's freeze-out of Miami Metro is no answer: exigency turns on the facts, not on which officer perceives them, and it was Special Agent Lundy's FBI that acted on the October 10 information. *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), is not to the contrary: it rejected a categorical "murder scene exception" for warrantless *searches of a home* — an intrusion of an entirely different order than two delayed location fixes assessed, as *Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141 (2013), requires, on the totality of these circumstances.

Petitioner's remaining answer is temporal: exigency is measured at inception, *McNeely*, 569 U.S. at 149–151, and the only governmental act was the October 8 fax, of which the October 10 fixes were mere "continuing yield." But this Court has never treated electronic monitoring as a single event fixed at installation: *Karo* assessed the beeper's monitoring episode by episode — condemning what revealed the home's interior, leaving the rest undisturbed — though the device was planted once. 468 U.S. at 714–718. Each transmitted fix is a discrete governmental acquisition, separately assessable for reasonableness, and on October 10 acquisition coincided with a grave, verifiable emergency. If the Court nonetheless treats the stream as indivisible, the same facts become dispositive at the remedial stage. Part IV, *infra*.

The stipulation that the SCA's emergency exception is unavailable does not foreclose this argument: statutory compliance and constitutional reasonableness are distinct inquiries, *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164, 176–178 (2008), and a stipulation about Doakes's October 8 certification cannot erase the objective facts of October 10.

### IV. IN ALL EVENTS, SUPPRESSION IS NOT THE REMEDY.

Even a Court persuaded that a search occurred should not reverse, for three independent reasons.

*First*, Congress spoke to remedy. The SCA provides civil damages against the offending actor, 18 U.S.C. § 2707, and conspicuously omits suppression from its remedial scheme; the courts of appeals uniformly hold that SCA violations do not warrant exclusion. *E.g.*, *United States v. Guerrero*, 768 F.3d 351, 358 (5th Cir. 2014). Section 2708 confirms the design, making those remedies exclusive for nonconstitutional violations of the chapter — so to the extent Petitioner grounds his privacy interest in § 2702's disclosure ban itself, that positive-law interest carries, by Congress's express command, only the positive-law remedies Congress chose. And if the claim is instead constitutional, the exclusionary rule's own limits take over.

*Second*, the exclusionary rule is reserved for cases where its deterrence benefits outweigh its substantial social costs. *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135, 141 (2009); *Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011). Doakes's false certification is genuinely deplorable — but the law already supplies his victims and his superiors the tools to answer it: § 2707 damages, professional discipline, and prosecution. Fourth Amendment doctrine governing every carrier, magistrate, and officer in the Nation should not be redesigned around its worst user. And as to the October 10 fixes — the only ones that located Petitioner — suppression would deter nothing: by that morning, an interrupted Butcher homicide and a fled killer gave law enforcement lawful access to the same data within minutes, through the exigent-circumstances doctrine, a truthful emergency certification, or a telephonic warrant, *see McNeely*, 569 U.S. at 154–155. Excluding evidence the police were by then entitled to obtain punishes the public for October 8 by suppressing October 10.

*Third*, the untainted evidence independently sustains this conviction. The park rangers discovered the crime scene *before and independent of* any location fix, *see Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988); *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984), and the trial maps rested on historical cell-site records distinct from the real-time stream. At the very most, Petitioner's arguments would support a remand for fruits-and-harmlessness analysis — not the overruling of *Miller*.

## CONCLUSION

For half a century, *Miller* and *Smith* have supplied a stable framework on which Congress, law enforcement, and the lower courts have built, and *Carpenter* proves this Court can mark digital-age limits without demolishing it. The Court should reaffirm *United States v. Miller*, hold that the limited real-time acquisition here was not a Fourth Amendment search — or, at minimum, that the material October 10 fixes were reasonable and that suppression would be unwarranted — and affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Thirteenth Circuit.
