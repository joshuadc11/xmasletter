## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether this Court should discard fifty years of settled Fourth Amendment doctrine by overruling *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976), and abolishing the third-party doctrine, where law enforcement — during an active manhunt for the deadliest serial killer in American history — obtained from a wireless carrier a brief, targeted stream of delayed location fixes, the only material two of which showed Petitioner's phone in the open Everglades and at a service station on a public highway.

## STATEMENT

Eighteen bodies lay on the floor of Bay Harbor, each the work of a single killer. By October 2007 the FBI had assumed control of the largest investigation in Miami's history, and the evidence indicated that the Bay Harbor Butcher was a law-enforcement insider still at large. On October 8, Detective James Doakes — acting on his own — faxed Verizon an emergency-disclosure form under 18 U.S.C. § 2702 requesting "current" location information for Petitioner's phone. The parties have stipulated that the statutory emergency exception does not apply, and the Government does not defend Doakes's certification. For the next several days, Verizon furnished location data, each fix delayed by thirty minutes.

On October 10, park rangers stumbled upon a murder and cover-up *in progress* in the Everglades, forensically matched to the Butcher's signature; the perpetrator had fled and was unlocated. That same day, two fixes showed Petitioner's phone in a remote part of the Everglades and then at a service station ten miles away on Florida State Road 9336. FBI agents — to whom Doakes relayed the information — located Petitioner at a Miami drive-thru and arrested him; a search of his trunk revealed knives, plastic wrap, and luminol, and agents recovered the body of Hector Estrada at the scene the rangers had already discovered. At trial, the Government's expert mapped "cell-site data provided by the wireless carrier," placing the phone "within a quarter mile" of the murder scene — the only quantified measure of accuracy in this record.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

This case arises not from idle surveillance but from the hunt for an active serial killer who, on the very day of the material location fixes, had been interrupted mid-murder and had vanished into the Everglades. Petitioner asks the Court to answer that record with a constitutional revolution: overrule *Miller*, abolish the third-party doctrine, and constitutionalize every subpoena for third-party records in the American economy.

Stare decisis forecloses that request. *Miller* and *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979), have governed for half a century; Congress built the Stored Communications Act on their foundation; and this Court reaffirmed them — expressly — only eight years ago in *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296, 316 (2018). Petitioner recites the overruling factors but never supplies what the Court would need before wielding them: a replacement rule. His regime would leave grand juries, tax authorities, and fraud investigators guessing whether each bank record or shipping manifest is now a warrant-only item.

Nor was the acquisition here a search. *Carpenter*'s holding rested on the retrospective, encyclopedic character of a week-long historical archive; the Court expressly reserved real-time CSLI, and the federal courts of appeals applying *Carpenter* have held that short-term, targeted real-time locating of a phone moving through public space is not a search. *United States v. Hammond*, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021); *United States v. Riley*, 858 F.3d 1012 (6th Cir. 2017). That conclusion follows from *United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983), and does not depend on the third-party doctrine at all: the fixes here revealed movements in open wetlands and on a public highway, nothing more.

Finally, even if a search occurred, the October 10 acquisitions — the only ones that mattered — were objectively reasonable under the exigent circumstances of an interrupted homicide, and suppression would in any event be an improper remedy for what is, at bottom, a statutory-process violation by a rogue officer whom Congress has given his victims other means to punish.

The judgment below should be affirmed.

## ARGUMENT

### I. STARE DECISIS FORECLOSES OVERRULING *MILLER* AND THE THIRD-PARTY DOCTRINE.

#### A. The doctrine rests on a sound and enduring principle.

The third-party doctrine flows from *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967): what a person "knowingly exposes" to others is not protected. *Miller* applied that principle to records a customer conveyed to his bank; *Smith* to numbers dialed to the phone company; *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), *Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293 (1966), and *California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988), apply the same insight elsewhere. The Fourth Amendment protects *the people's* papers and effects — not business records created and held by another for its own purposes.

#### B. Petitioner has no special justification — and no replacement rule.

Overruling precedent requires "special justification," not a present belief that the case was wrongly decided. *Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC*, 576 U.S. 446, 455 (2015). Petitioner answers that constitutional stare decisis is at its weakest, *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991) — true, but weakest is not nonexistent, and every factor this Court actually weighs, *see Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022), cuts against him.

**Recent reaffirmation.** *Carpenter* is not erosion; it is reaffirmation. The Court considered these very criticisms of *Miller* and *Smith*, declined to "disturb" either, and called its own holding "a narrow one." 585 U.S. at 316. Petitioner's claim that *Carpenter* "hollowed out" *Miller* inverts what the Court said it was doing eight years ago.

**Workability.** Petitioner calls *Carpenter*-plus-exceptions unworkable, but his alternative is no rule at all. He would "restore *Katz*'s ordinary reasonable-expectation inquiry, under which disclosure to a third party is one factor." That is a case-by-case sensitivity audit of every category of record held by every third party — bank ledgers, toll records, utility bills, shipping manifests — converting routine grand-jury and administrative subpoena practice, *see Oklahoma Press Publishing Co. v. Walling*, 327 U.S. 186 (1946), into perpetual constitutional litigation. Petitioner never explains how a grand jury investigating securities fraud obtains brokerage records under his regime. A party seeking to overrule a foundational precedent owes the Court a workable successor; Petitioner offers a mood.

**Reliance.** Congress legislated against *Miller* and *Smith* when it enacted the SCA, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2701–2712, calibrating process category by category. Petitioner says the Government has "no legitimate reliance" in unconstitutional practices — but that is the conclusion dressed as a premise. The reliance here is *legislative*: an entire statutory architecture, plus decades of tax, fraud, and securities enforcement, built on the settled understanding that compelled production of third-party business records is not a search.

#### C. Calibration belongs to Congress.

Statutes can be recalibrated as technology changes; constitutional rulings cannot. Congress remains free to layer protections above the constitutional floor — as it did in the SCA itself, and as it can again. A decision constitutionalizing all third-party records would freeze a one-size-fits-all warrant rule into the Fourth Amendment, displacing the nuanced, democratically accountable regime that already exists. *Carpenter* shows the Court can police genuine digital-age excesses with a scalpel; Petitioner demands a wrecking ball.

### II. THE LIMITED REAL-TIME ACQUISITION HERE WAS NOT A FOURTH AMENDMENT SEARCH.

#### A. *Carpenter*'s rationale does not extend to short-term, targeted, prospective locating — and Petitioner's *a fortiori* argument inverts it.

Petitioner argues that real-time tracking is *worse* than historical CSLI, so *Carpenter* governs *a fortiori*. That misreads what did the work in *Carpenter*. The Court's concern was the *retrospective archive*: a "detailed, encyclopedic, and effortlessly compiled" record letting police "travel back in time to retrace a person's whereabouts," compiled automatically as to every subscriber without any investigative predicate. 585 U.S. at 309–312. A prospective, targeted location fix has none of those features. It cannot reconstruct the past; it reaches only one suspect, upon a specific request; and it replicates what *Knotts* has always permitted — following a particular suspect's present movements — rather than conferring the time-machine omniscience that troubled the *Carpenter* Court. That is why *Carpenter* expressly declined to express a view on "real-time CSLI," *id.* at 316: the rationale does not translate, and the reservation would have been pointless if real-time tracking were covered *a fortiori*.

The federal courts of appeals agree. In *United States v. Hammond*, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021), the Seventh Circuit held post-*Carpenter* that hours of real-time CSLI used to locate a suspect traveling public highways was not a search, because *Knotts*, not *Carpenter*, supplies the rule for short-term tracking of public movements. In *United States v. Riley*, 858 F.3d 1012 (6th Cir. 2017), the Sixth Circuit reached the same conclusion for roughly seven hours of real-time tracking of a fugitive that revealed nothing about the interior of any protected space. Petitioner's authorities, by contrast, are state decisions — *Tracey*, *Pacheco*, *Almonor* — resting in substantial part on state constitutional guarantees that state courts remain free to read more broadly. On the federal question, the federal authority is against him.

As for duration: the Government does not pretend this was a single ping. Doakes received fixes over several days. But the stream was a sequence of discrete, thirty-minute-stale fixes on one suspect — and the only fixes this record discloses, and the only two that mattered, showed the phone in open wetlands and at a service station on a public road. Even counted whole, a days-long series of delayed fixes during a targeted manhunt sits comfortably within the territory *Carpenter* reserved, *see id.* at 310 n.3, and far from the seven-day historical archive it condemned. And the records the Government's expert actually mapped at trial were *historical cell-site data* spanning less than the *Carpenter* threshold — a distinct, sub-seven-day dataset within footnote 3's express reservation.

#### B. *Knotts* resolves this case without any help from *Miller*.

Even if the Court narrowed or retired the third-party doctrine, the judgment stands, because "[a] person traveling in an automobile on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another." *Knotts*, 460 U.S. at 281. The fixes located the phone in the Everglades — the archetypal open field, *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984); *Hester v. United States*, 265 U.S. 57 (1924) — and at a public service station, from which Doakes inferred a direction of travel visible to any motorist.

*United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984), marks the line, and confirms which side this case falls on: monitoring becomes a search when it *actually reveals* information about the interior of a private residence "that could not have been obtained by observation from outside," *id.* at 715. Petitioner's answer — that real-time pinging is "indifferent to the home's threshold" — converts *Karo*'s actual-revelation holding into a capability test this Court has never adopted. No fix here placed the phone in a home or any protected enclave. *Knotts*' reservation of "dragnet-type" continuous surveillance likewise does not assist Petitioner: a few delayed fixes on a single fleeing suspect is investigation, not dragnet.

#### C. The *Almonor* agency theory fails on this record and under this Court's cases.

Petitioner's strongest analytical move is that nothing was "voluntarily conveyed" because Verizon, at police command, *generated* the location data — acting as an agent, not a records custodian. Three answers.

*First*, the record does not show GPS activation. The only quantified accuracy in this record is the trial expert's "within a quarter mile" — tower-scale, not GPS-scale. "GPS data" appears only as the label in Petitioner's own suppression motion; what the carrier provided, and what was mapped at trial, was "cell-site data." A network necessarily knows, at every moment, which of its towers serves each powered handset — that knowledge *is* the provision of service. Reporting it, even on request, is a query of the carrier's own operational data, not the manufacture of new information.

*Second*, the agency theory proves too little. Petitioner says the Government cannot "outsource" *Karo*'s beeper. But the beeper cases themselves show why agency is beside the point: the *Knotts* beeper was a *government-installed* device — no voluntary conveyance whatsoever — and its monitoring on public roads still was not a search. The dispositive question under *Katz* is what the surveillance *revealed*, not the corporate identity of who relayed it. Where, as here, what was revealed is public movement, the conveyance debate is academic.

*Third*, to the extent voluntariness matters, it is secondary. The Government's lead submission is *Knotts*; the third-party doctrine independently supports the judgment as to records the carrier holds in the ordinary course, but the Court need not resolve how *Carpenter*'s voluntariness discussion, 585 U.S. at 315, maps onto prospective fixes in order to affirm.

#### D. Petitioner's rule has no limiting principle, and the statutory structure cuts against him.

If a thirty-minute-stale fix on a public highway is a warrant-only event, so is every effort to locate an abducted child's phone or an armed fugitive. Anticipating the structural argument that Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(b)(4) treats prospective tracking as warrant territory: that rule governs *government-installed tracking devices*, 18 U.S.C. § 3117. Congress addressed carrier-derived records separately, in the SCA, with calibrated lesser process and an emergency pathway — proof that Congress itself distinguishes the two, and assigned this category to the statutory, not constitutional, domain.

### III. ALTERNATIVELY, THE OCTOBER 10 ACQUISITIONS WERE REASONABLE UNDER THE EXIGENT-CIRCUMSTANCES DOCTRINE.

The reasonableness inquiry attaches to each governmental acquisition, and the only fixes that located Petitioner or figured in this prosecution were obtained on October 10. The Government does not defend Doakes's October 8 certification, and the Court may assume the October 8–9 stream was unlawful. But by October 10 the objective picture had transformed: rangers had interrupted a Butcher murder *in progress* that day; the killer had fled and was unlocated; eighteen prior victims attested to what delay would cost. An officer may act without a warrant where "the exigencies of the situation" make it objectively reasonable, *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452, 460 (2011); *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967), and the inquiry is objective — Doakes's motives are irrelevant, *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 404 (2006). Nor does it matter that the FBI had frozen Miami Metro out of the investigation: exigency turns on the facts, not on which officer perceives them, and it was Special Agent Lundy's FBI that acted on the October 10 information. *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), is not to the contrary: it rejected a categorical "murder scene exception" for warrantless *searches of a home* — an intrusion of an entirely different order than two delayed location fixes assessed, as *Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141 (2013), requires, on the totality of these circumstances.

The parties' stipulation that the SCA's statutory emergency exception is unavailable does not foreclose this argument. Statutory compliance and constitutional reasonableness are distinct inquiries, *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164, 176–178 (2008); the stipulation concerns the statutory standard governing the provider's disclosure, and it cannot erase the objective facts of October 10.

### IV. IN ALL EVENTS, SUPPRESSION IS NOT THE REMEDY.

Even a Court persuaded that a search occurred should not reverse, for three independent reasons.

*First*, Congress spoke to remedy. The SCA's remedial scheme provides civil damages, 18 U.S.C. § 2707, and declares its remedies exclusive, § 2708 — conspicuously omitting suppression. The courts of appeals uniformly hold that SCA violations do not warrant exclusion. *E.g.*, *United States v. Guerrero*, 768 F.3d 351, 358 (5th Cir. 2014). And to the extent Petitioner grounds a privacy interest in § 2702's disclosure ban itself, a positive-law interest comes with positive-law remedies — the ones Congress chose.

*Second*, the exclusionary rule is reserved for cases where its deterrence benefits outweigh its substantial social costs. *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135, 141 (2009); *Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011). Doakes's false certification is genuinely deplorable — but the law already supplies his victims and his superiors the tools to answer it: § 2707 damages, professional discipline, and prosecution. Fourth Amendment doctrine governing every carrier, magistrate, and officer in the Nation should not be redesigned around its worst user.

*Third*, the untainted evidence independently sustains this conviction. The park rangers discovered the crime scene *before and independent of* any location fix, *see Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988); *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984), and the trial maps rested on historical cell-site records distinct from the real-time stream. At the very most, Petitioner's arguments would support a remand for fruits-and-harmlessness analysis — not the overruling of *Miller*.

## CONCLUSION

For half a century, *Miller* and *Smith* have supplied a stable framework on which Congress, law enforcement, and the lower courts have built, and *Carpenter* proves this Court can mark digital-age limits without demolishing it. The Court should reaffirm *United States v. Miller*, hold that the limited real-time acquisition here was not a Fourth Amendment search — or, at minimum, that the material October 10 fixes were reasonable and that suppression would be unwarranted — and affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Thirteenth Circuit.
