                                     ARGUMENT

       I.     THIS COURT SHOULD ENTER A DEFAULT JUDGMENT AGAINST
              DEFENDANTS AS A SANCTION FOR THE PRODUCTION OF
              FABRICATED EVIDENCE.

       Federal district courts possess the inherent power “to protect the orderly

 administration of justice and to preserve the dignity of the tribunal.” Kleiner v. First

 Nat’l Bank of Atlanta, 751 F.2d 1193, 1209 (11th Cir. 1985) (citing Roadway

 Express v. Piper, 447 U.S. 752, 764-65 (1980)). “This power includes ‘the ability

 to fashion an appropriate sanction for conduct which abuses the judicial

 process[.]’” VIP Auto Glass, Inc. v. GEICO Gen. Ins. Co., No. 16-CV-2012, 2018

 WL 3649638, at *3 (M.D. Fla. Jan. 3, 2018) (quoting Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.,

 501 U.S. 32, 44-45 (1991)). The inherent powers doctrine is “most often invoked

 where a party commits perjury or destroys or doctors evidence.” Access

 Innovators, LLC v. Usha Martin Ltd., No. 09-CV-2893, 2010 WL 11508119, at *1

 (N.D. Ga. Apr. 28, 2010) (quoting Qantum Commc’ns. Corp. v. Star Broad., Inc.,
                                           13
Case 3:24-cv-00963-WWB-SJH       Document 145      Filed 04/13/26    Page 14 of 26 PageID
                                       1766



 473 F. Supp. 2d 1249, 1269 (S.D. Fla. 2007)).

       False evidence need not be introduced at trial to be sanctionable, see, e.g.,

 Forsberg v. Pefanis, 634 F. App’x 676, 679 (11th Cir. 2015); rather, the production

 of fabricated documents in discovery is sufficient to justify case-ending sanctions,

 see, e.g., Franklin Livestock, Inc. v. Boehringer Ingelheim Vetmedica, Inc., 251 F.

 Supp. 3d 962, 971 (E.D.N.C. 2017) , aff’d, 721 F. App’x 263 (4th Cir. 2018); Garcia

 v. Berkshire Life Ins. Co. Of Am., 569 F.3d 1174, 1182 (10th Cir. 2009). As the

 Eleventh Circuit noted, “[i]t would be strange to require a district court to wait until

 false evidence is introduced at trial before sanctions are appropriate, all the while

 wasting the time and resources of the court and the opposing party.” Forsberg,

 634 F. App'x at 679. In short, “false evidence is sufficient cause for sanctions[.]”

 Id. Indeed, “[c]ourts faced with the fabrication of evidence by a party frequently

 impose the sanction of dismissal or default.” Art Remedy, LLC v. Lana Moes Art,

 LLC, No. 18-61912-CIV, 2019 WL 13063496, at *4 (S.D. Fla. Sept. 26, 2019)

 (collecting cases). However, before entering a case-terminating sanction, a court

 must find that: (1) Defendants acted willfully or in bad faith; (2) Plaintiffs were

 prejudiced by Defendants’ conduct; and (3) lesser sanctions would not serve the

 goals of punishment and deterrence. See Telectron, Inc. v. Overhead Door Corp.,

 116 F.R.D. 107, 131 (S.D. Fla. 1987).

       In this case, all three elements are satisfied.

              A.     Defendants acted willfully and in bad faith.

       “Bad faith exists when the court finds that a fraud has been practiced upon
                                           14
Case 3:24-cv-00963-WWB-SJH       Document 145      Filed 04/13/26    Page 15 of 26 PageID
                                       1767



 it, or that the very temple of justice has been defiled, or where a party or attorney

 knowingly or recklessly raises a frivolous argument, delays or disrupts the

 litigation, or hampers the enforcement of a court order.” Qantum Commc’ns Corp.,

 473 F. Supp. 2d at 1268-69 (cleaned up). Courts have found that fabricating and

 submitting knowingly false evidence constitutes willful and bad faith conduct. See

 Am. Rena Int'l Corp. v. Sis-Joyce Int'l Co., No. CV 12-6972, 2015 WL 12732433,

 at *23 (C.D. Cal. Dec. 14, 2015).

       In Garcia v. Berkshire Life Ins. Co. of Am., the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals

 affirmed a district court’s dismissal of a plaintiff’s case, following the trial court’s

 finding that the plaintiff had fabricated at least four self-serving documents that she

 produced during discovery to support her claims. 569 F.3d 1174, 1177-1182 (10th

 Cir. 2009). Similarly, in Franklin Livestock, Inc., the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals

 affirmed a district court’s dismissal of a plaintiff’s claims, when the plaintiff

 knowingly produced fabricated business records in discovery. 251 F. Supp. 3d at

 962, aff’d, 721 F. App’x 263 (4th Cir. 2018). In Roche Diagnostics Corp. v. Priority

 Healthcare Corp., the Northern District of Alabama entered default judgment as a

 sanction against defendants that produced forged invoices in discovery. 8 No. 18-

 CV-01479, 2020 WL 2308319, at *11 (N.D. Ala. May 8, 2020).

       Applying these principles to the case at bar, a default judgment is warranted.

 The evidence supports a finding that Defendants, in bad faith, fabricated evidence

 relating to a pivotal issue in this case, namely, the compensation terms in Plaintiffs’

 agreements with FR8. The Altered Agreements were produced bearing Plaintiffs’

 signatures at the end (when Plaintiffs never signed those Altered Agreements) and

 were identified in discovery responses produced by all three Defendants as

 evidence to support their contention that Plaintiffs are not entitled to 88% of the

 revenue for each load transported for FR8. These discovery responses have not

 been amended.9

       The critical nature of the fabricated evidence supports the severity of the

 sanction requested. See Roche Diagnostics Corp., 2020 WL 2308319, at *7 (“The

 severity of [a case-ending] sanction is warranted in large part because the

 doctored evidence cuts to the very essence of [plaintiff’s] substantive claims.”).

 Defendants produced self-serving fabricated evidence relating to a central issue in

 this case, compensation under the Independent Contractor Agreements, and

 specifically identified the Altered Agreements in their respective responses to

 Plaintiffs’ discovery requests seeking the evidence to support their contention that

 Plaintiffs are not entitled to 88% of the revenue per load transported. Because

              B.     Plaintiffs are prejudiced by Defendants’ fabrication of

       The Eleventh Circuit has recognized that prejudice from falsified evidence

 can consist of having to “consum[e] substantial resources to respond to and ‘undo’

 the deceitful party.” Forsberg, 634 F. App'x at 680 (cleaned up). Further, “falsified

 evidence substantially prejudices an opposing party by casting doubt on the

 veracity of all of the culpable party’s submissions throughout litigation.” Garcia, 569

 F.3d at 1180. When presented with fabricated evidence, “[t]he prejudiced party is

 forced either to attempt independent corroboration of each submission, at

 substantial expense of time and money, or to accept the real possibility that those

 discovery documents submitted by the opposing party are inaccurate.” Id. Not only

 does the alteration of evidence prejudice the victimized party, it also “prejudices

 the court and its administration of the judicial process to the extent it introduces

 uncertainty, requires additional investigation into the fabrication, and undermines

 the validity of the litigation process.” Martin v. SGT, Inc., No. 19-cv-00289, 2022

 WL 17975993, at *16 (D. Utah Dec. 28, 2022).

       The production of the Altered Agreements prejudiced Plaintiffs, as Plaintiffs’

 attorneys and their staff spent significant time and effort investigating the nature

 and origin of the Altered Agreements to determine whether they were authentic.

 Plaintiffs’ attorneys also had to consult with, and pay for, a forensic expert to

 confirm the Altered Agreements were manipulated.

              C.     Lesser sanctions would not serve the goals of punishment
                     and deterrence.

       Case-terminating sanctions are particularly appropriate where, as here, a

 party submits fabricated evidence. See, e.g., Roche Diagnostics Corp., 2020 WL

 2308319, at *7; VIP Auto Glass, 2018 WL 3649638, at *4-5 (“Numerous courts

 have dismissed claims where a party has fabricated evidence or committed

 perjury.”). A lesser sanction, such as the exclusion of the fabricated evidence,

 would not be enough to deter further discovery misconduct by Defendants. See,

 e.g., Carroll-Harris v. Wilkie, No. 17-CV-11711, 2019 WL 2205851, at *7 (E.D.

 discovery would not be appropriate). Assessing fees and costs, without more,

 would not “sufficiently address [Plaintiffs’] concern about the veracity of other

 documents disclosed” by Defendants. Id. Exclusion of the Altered Agreements

 would also be an “insufficient sanction” for purposes of deterrence, as “litigants

 could infer that they have everything to gain, and nothing to lose by manufacturing

 evidence.” Id. at *8 (internal quotations omitted). Allowing Defendants’ misconduct

 to go unpunished would not serve the ends of justice, and it would not deter similar

 wrongdoing in the future. A default judgment is the appropriate sanction in this

 case. See, e.g., Access Innovators, 2010 WL 11508119, at *3 (“When a party

 fabricates a document or provides false evidence relating to a key issue in a case,

 courts have made clear that the appropriate sanction is the ultimate sanction of

 dismissal.”).
